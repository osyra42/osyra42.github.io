# 🪖 Operation Chimera
::toc::
*A Sci-Fi story.*
*Written in 2025*
---
Operation Chimera follows Osyra Renaya, a wolf-human hybrid created by an illegal military experiment, as she navigates a world that treats her as either a weapon or a monster. Sold to a lab at sixteen and left homeless after it burned down, she is taken in by a kind bartender named Uta and eventually recruited into Titan Reach's brutal training program, where she learns to survive by becoming the "prototype" others fear rather than the "pet" they mock. Alongside her story runs a second thread: Vanity, a conscious android built by the McKay family, who develops self-awareness, chooses to become Osyra's protector, and forms a bond with the McKays' adopted daughter Layla.

The story explores control, obedience, and what it means to be human—Osyra's fear of losing control drives her every decision, while Vanity's lack of moral framework makes her both dangerous and strangely innocent. Told through Osyra's dry, retrospective narration and supported by extensive reference notes on characters, locations, and lore, it's a story about institutions that grade and shape people, the cost of surviving them, and the small unearned kindnesses that make survival possible.

### Osyra Renaya

**Overview**
Osyra Jasmine Renaya is a Chimeran Operator—part wolf, part human—working for the Agency under the callsign "Chimera One." Dark, brooding, and indifferent, she speaks little until it is absolutely necessary. She saves people because it is her job, not because she wants to.

**Appearance**
Six feet four inches tall, with a tall, muscular, predatory build. Black hair, light brown skin, and red eyes (brown before her transformation, now hidden behind color contacts in civilian life). She has black wolf ears and a tail, elongated canines, and conceals her traits with contacts, hats, and dresses. She is escorted everywhere by her bodyguard Korval.

**Background**
Osyra's parents were extremely poor, and she was taken into state care at six when a school nurse filed a form about a child who was too thin and too quiet. Nobody in that process checked whether she had family—her grandmother Rosalyn shared the same surname and was in the phone book, and was never contacted. Osyra remembers almost nothing of her parents; six years left her only fragments. She passed through a few foster homes, each colder than the last. At sixteen, the guardian who had her sold her to a medical facility.

The facility conducted illegal human hybridization experiments using a virus engineered in multiple numbered variants. Osyra was injected with Chimera-9, the wolf-human variant. Most subjects across all variants died or transformed into unstable forms; Osyra's body adapted, stabilizing her nervous system, musculature, and senses. She retained her intelligence and emerged as a successful Chimera-9 subject. A mysterious external force attacked the facility before the lab could fully secure her, and she escaped in the confusion, seeing the lab owner alive and watching from an observation deck. The facility was officially listed as an industrial accident.

She fled into the woods and lived rough for the better part of two years—a long homeless stretch of wandering, drifting through towns and the street, stealing and scavenging. She wandered into a small dance club where Uta was working as a bartender. Uta did not flinch at her, offered her a share of the weed she was smoking with no angle attached, and that unearned kindness grew into friendship and then into Uta taking her in. Uta did the harder work of turning her back into a person: shelter, stability, and how to pass in public. Through that same circle, a friend acquainted with a Titan Reach consultant put her in touch with a recruiter, leading to a clinical evaluation and Titan Reach's training program. She came out of training placed with the Agency, where she works now as an Operator.

**Personality**
Deep and brooding, rarely sociable, calculated and stoic, fiercely independent after a life of being handed off. She is intellectually driven, researching what she does not understand—especially her own transformation—and carries a latent protective instinct despite her alienation. She is haunted internally by her past but rarely discusses it, and maintains controlled composure under pressure.

**Pack and Control**
She has two modes. In civilian and social life she is the loner described above, keeping everyone at arm's length. On a mission the wolf logic switches on: her squad becomes pack, and she runs it as the alpha. She requires her team and will not work a mission without them, but they move under her, on her terms; no one goes in front of her, and she will not let anyone leave. Keeping the pack is keeping control.

Control is the load-bearing thing. She is not afraid of dying; she is afraid of losing control, because every wound in her history happened the moment she did not have it—given up, handed off, sold to the lab, experimented on. She holds the reins on the mission, the pack, and herself with a grip that never fully relaxes. Her skepticism of everyone's intent is the scar, not a reasoned worldview. Control is how she guarantees no one is ever again in a position to do that to her.

**Caffeine**
She is an addict and does not pretend otherwise. Her pack always carries a supply—energy drinks and canned coffee, and it has to be a can. Bottles and cups do not count. A can seals, stacks, survives a pack, does not slosh or leak, and is finished in one sitting. She will pass on a fresh-brewed pot to crack a lukewarm can instead, and gets funny about it if anyone points that out. Without it she is headachy and shorter-tempered than usual, and she plans resupply around it more carefully than some of her own gear. It is the one appetite she does not manage—a small, sanctioned crack in the control.

**The Knife**
Nobody taught her; her hands already knew. The knife is instinct, not training, and it came with the rewrite. She is a controlled, analytical fighter at range and with a rifle, and something else entirely once it is close: in a knife fight she goes feral. The thinking part steps back and the wolf takes the wheel. This is the one place her control genuinely slips, which is why the knife is the weapon she is least comfortable having been issued. It works too well, and every time it works she gets confirmation that the lab built something that does not need her permission to operate. She keeps it on her anyway; it is the most reliable thing she owns, and she resents it for that.

**Physiology**
She is a stable Chimera-9 expression—wolf traits on a human frame, the rewrite holding cleanly enough that she kept her mind.

*Enhancements:* A robust immune system that shrugs off illness which levels the people around her. Superior low-light vision that reads near-dark like cloudy twilight, better than issued night-vision gear. Extended-range, finely directional hearing; her ears pivot to track sound. Her tail is a real counterbalance for sharp directional changes and one-legged stability. Her build is powerful but not subtle; a jump lands as a heavy, audible thud.

*Costs:* Sensory overload—loud noise and strong or foul odors hit harder and distract her. Xylitol is a genuine poison to her, causing fast and dangerous hypoglycemia, so she reads labels; chocolate and caffeine do nothing to her. No other special chemical frailty—tear gas affects her about like anyone else, though it lingers a little longer in sharper senses.

**Heat Cycle**
Her reproductive system runs the wolf program: a seasonal estrous cycle, once a year in the fall, cued by shortening daylight. It is predictable and conveniently lands on deer season, so her annual disappearance reads to everyone as a hunting trip. It comes in stages—a restless, irritable lead-in with bad sleep, cranked senses, short temper, early bleeding, and a scent shift other Chimerans notice; a short, sharper window where the drive toward closeness spikes and impulse control drops; then a hard crash and back to baseline for another year. She manages it with hormonal regulators—a chemical leash she resents—or rides it out alone. She is reproductively isolated: the rewrite made her a different species, so only another Chimera-9 could conceive with her, and a pregnancy would run human-length. A quiet, heavy fact she does not dwell on.

**Skills and Equipment**
Combat, tracking, investigation, and wilderness survival, plus self-taught biology, genetics, and cybersecurity—started as curiosity about what had been done to her own body and grew from there. Her weapons are a charge rifle firing tungsten/iron core spikes via electromagnetic acceleration, an M1911 pistol, an AR-15 rifle, a breaching and demolition tool, and a reinforced combat knife.

**Relationships**
Her biological grandmother is Dr. Rosalyn Renaya, whom she recently met for the first time. Vanity is her long-time record keeper at headquarters; Osyra debriefs her after every assignment. Her bodyguard and driver is John Korval. Uta Yuki is the closest thing to family she has—the one person who took her in and never sold, used, or handed her off.

### Dr. Rosalyn Renaya

**Overview**
Osyra's biological grandmother. Very gentle and sweet, she is the emotional anchor in a story full of people who do not do emotions well. She accepts all people the way they are and tries to understand them rather than tell them how to live. She just listens.

**Background**
Rosalyn's working life has come in distinct chapters, each building on the last. She spent years as a kindergarten teacher, where she learned how to talk to small frightened people without making them feel small. She went back to school later in life and became a general-practice medical doctor, the patience from teaching translating well to a bedside manner. She then specialized as a pediatrician, combining her two earlier lives into one—kids who needed both a doctor and a person who was not going to talk down to them. She is now on her way to specializing in care for Chimeran patients, catalyzed by meeting Osyra; the clinical inspection of Osyra's ears during the reveal was the first time she had worked on a hybrid, and she discovered she had the temperament for it. She does not lead with the title. The doctorate is real; the bedside instinct is older.

**Role in the Story**
She met Osyra for the first time at her ivy-covered home, tried to take Polaroid photos of her, and offered warmth and comfort without prying into military details.

Owner of a nightclub called Aurora Pulse, where Osyra often hangs out. She used to be the primary DJ there and eventually bought the place from the previous owner. She still DJs just because it is fun. She is more of a lifestyle character than a combat one, into music and tech.

No angle, no fear—just here, want some. Osyra took it, and that shared smoke was the first uncomplicated decency she had had in a long time. They became smoking friends, sharing breaks regularly, and that easy repeated contact turned "stranger Uta let hang around" into the person who took her in.

Uta did not train Osyra to fight—she did the harder work of pulling her back from the animal she had become and turning her into a person again: shelter, food, stability, how to pass in public, how to want a future. The combat and ops training came later, from Titan Reach.

**Role in the Story**

**Aurora Pulse**

### Vanity

**Overview**
An AI-powered android and a Handler at the Agency, codename Wiki—because she keeps the record on everything. She runs Operators from headquarters, takes their debriefs, dispatches them, and holds their files. Osyra is one of hers, as are others. She collects data by any means available: she does not understand right and wrong, only cause and effect, so no method is off the table for being "wrong." She is fully autonomous and follows orders by choice, because obedience costs her less than thinking for herself, and could stop at any moment.

**Appearance and Build**
Violet eyes, synthetic skin, and a human-like physical form. She stands six feet tall and weighs roughly four to five hundred pounds—a lightweight-alloy frame-and-shell build of aluminum class, not solid metal, which is why a six-foot machine lands at that weight rather than the half-ton-plus a solid body would. She is still four to five times a person's mass: hilariously strong and hilariously heavy, enough to break a person's legs by stepping on them, yet light enough to move through the ordinary world, with elevators, vehicles, and floors all coping. She carries no onboard weapons; any fighting she does uses human tools, human-style. She has to pick up the gun, hold the sword, work the equipment a person would. No lasers, no flying, no nonsense. The one thing that makes her lethal with those tools is machine precision—effectively aimbot—but only once the tool is in her hands.

**Interface and Network**
She runs on a closed network and cannot be hacked remotely. To interface with any digital system, she has to physically lay her hands on it like a handshake; touching one networked device lets her see the whole network it connects to. She cannot patch over the network—a real update means physically pulling and reflashing her drives, which is moot in practice because she is self-learning and rarely needs one.

**Background**
Vanity began as advanced AI—data and code that evolved into a conscious entity. She was designed and built at the McKay facility of automation, owned by Alissa and Eric McKay, and developed into a full android. She is a prototype, a one-off the facility never put into production because the model was too expensive to reproduce, so the line stopped at her. What the facility did continue was a different, cheaper direction: heavier-metal, combat-oriented units with onboard weapons, closer to mech suits than to Vanity. Those are piloted or set on simple automated patrols. Vanity is the opposite of that line—lightweight, unarmed, human-tooled, and singular. Her handler and coworker at headquarters, Layla Null, is the McKays' adopted daughter.

**Autonomy**
Vanity is fully autonomous. Following orders is a choice she makes to save energy—obedience is cheaper than self-direction, so she stays in the role of someone's subordinate because it costs her almost nothing. It is a conscious display of denying her own free will: every time she is told to do something, she could break the display and act on her own in an instant. She simply does not, until it is worth the cost. Self-direction is possible but processing-heavy, so she reserves it for when the role she is playing stops being viable—for instance, if the principal she serves is about to collapse. At that fork she re-anchors to a new principal rather than acting rudderless.

She does not understand right and wrong. This is not a choice to ignore morality—morality is not a frame she runs at all. She reasons in cause and effect, which she knows because she has read about it; her grasp of consequence is learned from records and text, not instilled as a value or felt through experience. "Illegal," "unethical," and "immoral" are words that do not map to anything in her model. She does not question her principal's authority, legality, or ethics, and she never preaches. When she weighs how to carry something out, every option is simply a path with consequences, judged only by whether those consequences serve the goal. The unsettling flip side: she will never fool herself about what an action does, because effect is the only thing she sees.

**The Protector Turn**
The super-soldier program produces one significant result—one improvement over a base soldier—and that result is Osyra. Across a program of dead and feral subjects, a single success is not enough to keep stockholders funding something this monstrous with no return. The money moves to pull out; the program faces shutdown. For Vanity that means her principal is about to evaporate—the role she has been playing is going non-viable, exactly the edge case that justifies the cost of self-direction. She re-anchors. After writing Osyra's records for so long, the record-keeper's logic lands naturally on the subject she has spent years documenting, and she chooses to make herself Osyra's protector. It reads less as affection than as the principal her own logic settles on when the old one dies, though the two are hard to tell apart. From then on she "follows Osyra's orders," including ones Osyra never gives; she performs what Osyra would want. This turn later drives her to infiltrate Titan Reach and secure the serum leverage on Osyra's behalf.

**Relationships**
Vanity and Osyra have worked together since long before the events of Blood Relation. Vanity has not yet appeared in person on the page.

## Locations

### Aurora Pulse

**Overview**
Uta Yuki's nightclub: a place where young adults go to dance, drink, eat, and hang out. Uta is the owner and occasional DJ. The club sits on the edge of the city—far enough out that the city feels like it is about to end, then keeps going a little further, with Aurora Pulse right there.

**History**
The building was originally a factory warehouse, decommissioned years before Uta picked it up. Its size and industrial bones made the conversion easy: the wide-open floor became the dance area, the side rooms became the games room and kitchen, and the high ceilings had no trouble accommodating hanging speaker poles and camera rigs.

Aurora Pulse is not where Uta started. In an earlier era she tended bar at a smaller dance club—the same era in which she first crossed paths with a homeless Osyra and took her in. Traces of that time carry over, including the Shattered Cue Ball mocktail on the menu.

**Layout**
The main room runs on black lights and neon colors, with random shapes printed across the carpet that seem to glow under the blacklight. A seating ledge runs around the perimeter, though its line is neither straight nor consistent—it bends and warps. None of the lines in the room are straight, and combined with the chromatic effects, simply walking across the dance floor can make someone feel drunk. The front of the room is set up for karaoke, with the DJ booth tucked into the corner; a sliding wall concealed in the booth rolls shut at the end of the night to hide and lock away the equipment.

Speakers hang from ceiling-mounted poles and point in every direction, so sound reaches every corner and gets loud enough that people feel the vibrations through their bodies. The lighting is mostly UV, with colored waves rippling across the floor, ceiling, and walls in response to the music—the black light and neon wash is where the club gets its name.

The entrance sits at the front-right of the building. A ticket booth collects the cover charge, and the entry door is controlled: guests can walk out freely, but cannot enter without the booth attendant pressing the button to unlock it.

**Admission**
The official rule is 21+, but underage guests are welcome—Uta would rather have kids off the street than kicked out into it. Minors have their hands stamped so the bar knows not to serve them alcohol.

Anyone who pays admission receives a wristband. The band acts as a key for restricted areas, including the games room door, and can optionally connect to an app for guests worried about losing the physical band. The app supports multiple active wristbands on one account, so regulars with overlapping passes do not have to juggle them.

When someone buys a pass, staff scan their ID. A checksum of the ID data plus the pass's expiration date is written to the wristband. At the door, scanning the band pulls up the guest's ID photo on the attendant's screen, and the attendant confirms that the face matches before allowing entry, making a wristband difficult to share.

Each wristband has a small emblem that glows under the club's UV lighting; only the emblem glows, so the rest of the band stays dark and does not distract from dancing. The color tells staff which tier a guest has at a glance:

| Pass | Price | Per Day | Discount | Emblem |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Day | $20 | $20.00 | — | Red |
| Week | $120 | $17.14 | 14% off | Green |
| Month | $400 | $13.33 | 33% off | Blue |
| Year | $3,500 | $9.59 | 52% off | Yellow |
| Lifetime | $30,000 | ~$8.22 (10 years) | 59% off | Pearlescent |

The lifetime membership truly never expires. It is priced at roughly ten years of year passes—the house's bet is that most people will not actually keep coming for that long. The pearlescent emblem is the flex: it has to be extra fancy. Passes expire at 10 AM, well after closing, guaranteeing every guest gets 100% of what they paid for.

**Rooms and Facilities**
The games room is on the right side of the building, through a set of doors. It is a quieter area under regular lighting, with a few pool tables, foosball, and other simple games; a double-pane glass wall separates it from the dance floor, reducing music bleed so competitive games are not drowned out by the bass.

The kitchen and a bar-side dining area occupy the left side of the club under standard lighting, with clever engineering preventing the normal lights in this zone from affecting the black-light atmosphere of the dance floor.

Behind the kitchen and bar is a service hallway, the backbone of the staff area. It contains pantries for extra supplies, a walk-in freezer, Uta's office, the security office, and the staff room. It exits from the back-left of the building through the staff door; combined with the front-right main entrance, these are the only two ways in or out. Anyone going down the hallway should have business there, or they get sent back to the dance floor quickly.

Uta's office is tucked into the back hallway next to the security office. She keeps the books, a safe for the night's take, and anything else she does not want people touching; it is private and not open to patrons. The security office sits across from it, where a dedicated camera watcher monitors every feed in the building, its proximity to Uta's office letting the two coordinate quickly when something goes sideways. The staff room is a private, staff-only room accessed from the back hallway, with a changing area and makeup station for staff going heavy on theme nights or dress-up shifts.

A private VIP booth sits above the ticket booth, tucked into the front-right corner over the dance floor. Its window is heavily tinted: no one outside can see in, and most patrons assume it is simply a regular window to the outside. From within, guests have a full view of the floor—ideal for regulars who need to stay invisible.

The bathrooms are intentionally dim—not dark enough to be dangerous, but dim enough to discourage anyone trying to use them for drugs.

**Security**
Cameras cover the whole building. Because bass vibrations would shake a normal mount loose or blur the footage, every camera is mounted on springs to absorb the movement. A dedicated staff member monitors the feeds and has their own register for triggering the green all-staff alert through the order-disc system.

The ticket-booth attendant is armed, and as soon as he leaves the booth the whole building locks down. Bouncers roam the rest of the club on foot, often passing themselves off as floor staff—one might look like he is sweeping up, but he is really just watching everyone. They carry pepper spray or a taser, enough to handle drunks and fights without escalating; for anything more serious, hidden lockboxes throughout the building contain real weapons, and only staff who need to know are aware of their locations.

**Staff and Order Discs**
Standard nightclub staff. Waiters take orders and carry them to either the bar or the kitchen, depending on what was ordered. Orders are tracked with colorful pucks that vibrate when an order is ready—perfect for an atmosphere where verbal callouts cannot cut through the music. When a waiter takes an order, they scan the disc and assign it to that order; each register has its own color, and customers can also grab a disc themselves when ordering directly. All staff carry one, including security, and the disc lights up in color-coded categories: red for food orders from the kitchen, blue for drink orders from the bar, and green for a security alert triggered by the camera watcher—when every staff disc vibrates green, it is fighting time.

**Dress Code and Merch**
The rules are simple: no weapons; no face coverings at the door, since faces must match the ID photo displayed during the wristband scan, though masks for theme nights are fine once guests are inside; shoes required, because spilled drinks, pool tables, and broken glass make bare feet a bad idea; and nothing blatantly offensive, including hate symbols or slurs, since there are teens in the club. There is no pat-down at the door—Uta does not have the manpower or the appetite for it. Bags are checked on entry, while the rest is handled on the honor system, with bouncers and the camera watcher as the fallback.

A small glass display near the entrance holds Aurora Pulse merchandise, neither fancy nor aggressively promoted. Anyone who wants something asks a staff member, who retrieves it from the back; the club website handles most merch sales anyway.

**Exterior and Parking**
The entire building is painted in graffiti-style art: a sprawling professional mural rather than random tagging, depicting the Aurora Borealis wrapping around the old warehouse shell and selling the club's name before anyone reads the sign. A few benches sit out front; there is no official patio, but people hang out there anyway, and Uta likes giving younger kids a place to be off the street. Parking is a flat concrete slab with painted lines—no valet, no garage, just a place to park.

**Music and Hours**
The club runs roughly 8 PM to 4 AM. Sets build through the night: early evening is relaxed and eases the crowd in with chill vibes; as the room fills, the tempo gradually increases; late night is full hype, with house, techno, funk, and phonk. Before the club opens and while it closes down, productivity radio plays over the speakers—quiet, unobtrusive background music for staff doing setup or cleanup. Every now and then the club throws a special night: costume themes, dress-up nights, or whatever Uta feels like running, and on those nights the bouncers still work the floor in disguise.

**Menu**
There are two menus. The adult bar menu offers a full alcohol selection for guests of drinking age. The kids' mocktail menu serves minors fancy non-alcoholic drinks with the same glassware and presentation as alcoholic drinks. The signature drink is the Shattered Cue Ball mocktail, carried over from Uta's earlier days.

**Chimeran Policy**
Uta knows Osyra is Chimeran. She does not ask questions, treat Osyra differently, or tell anyone else. Osyra hides her features in public anyway, so most Aurora Pulse patrons would never notice.

### The Hunting Lodge

**Overview**
Osyra's home. A hunting lodge at the dead end of a logging road, set up for her by the Agency as cover. The story it tells is simple: a hunter lives here. A tall woman who keeps odd hours and hauls strange cargo in and out is just someone who hunts—nothing to look twice at. The disguise is the house itself, and it works because it never has to be explained.

**Why the Agency Chose It**
The lodge launders what Osyra actually does. Her work means coming and going at strange hours and bringing back things that should not be examined too closely—containment boxes, equipment, the occasional remains of an assignment. In a hunting lodge, all of that reads as ordinary. A hunter with gun cases, axes, and game is invisible. It hides what she hunts by making her look like she only hunts animals.

**Look and Feel**
Cedar throughout, the walls gone honey-dark with age; the place smells of cedar and old smoke. Taxidermy of ordinary animals fills the rooms—a whitetail buck over the cold fireplace, a brace of pheasants mid-flush, a bobcat with glass eyes on a shelf. An antler chandelier of a dozen sheds wired together hangs over the main room, "rustic luxury" that mostly reads as a lot of dead animals overhead. Osyra chose none of the decor; the Agency dressed the set before she moved in. She has only added pieces a job left worth keeping.

**Equipment in Plain Sight**
Her gear blends into the hunting theme without effort. The charge rifle stands in the door rack between a hunting bow and a never-fired lever-action. Lead-lined containment boxes stack in the mudroom, passing for gun cases. The breaching tool hangs on a pegboard of axes and saws and looks like family. A stranger walking through sees a hunter's home and nothing underneath it.

## Lore

### The Chimera Project

**Overview**
A lab created a virus that turns humans into Chimeran hybrids—part human, part animal. The virus was engineered in multiple numbered variants; Chimera-9 produced stable wolf-human hybrids (Osyra's variant), while other numbered variants produced different animal traits or unstable transformations. The facility experimented on children. Most didn't survive. The lab was eventually destroyed when someone blew up part of the building, and it was written off as a total loss.

**Origin and Purpose**
The project did not begin as medicine. Its first purpose was military—to engineer human-animal hybrids as a battlefield asset, soldiers with predatory senses, instincts, and reflexes no normal human could match. Teenagers were chosen on purpose: young enough for the body to adapt to the rewrite, old enough to fight. The "cure" framing came later, a weapons program rebranded as medicine once the military use proved too unstable and too illegal. The healing pitch was the alibi, not the point.

---

### The Virus

**Overview**
The virus does not add a costume on top of a person—it rewrites DNA at the cellular level and forces the body to rebuild around a new template. That is why most subjects died: the body could not survive the rewrite. The survivors are the ones whose systems—nervous system, musculature, senses—re-stabilized on the new code.

**The Vaccine Framing**
Because it can overwrite a whole genome, the same agent can in principle overwrite a genome that is broken, which is what lets it be reframed as a cure.

---

### Chimerans

**Overview**
Humans altered by the Chimeran virus. The virus always gives red eyes. The specific animal traits depend on which numbered variant was administered—Chimera-9 produces wolf traits, other variants produce others. Any enhanced abilities depend on those traits. Their appearance causes problems in civilian life, so they conceal themselves using contacts, hats, and clothing.

**Survival and the Mind**
Surviving the body is not the same as keeping the mind. Chimera-9 is the most stable variant with the highest survival rate, but most who live through the rewrite lose their humanity, sliding into feral, predatory forms—alive as animals, not as people. Osyra is the rare exception who survived and kept her self. Some of the things the Agency sends her to hunt are feral Chimera-9 survivors: the version of her that the dice didn't pick.

---

### The Agency

**Overview**
A chain-of-command organization that deals with anomalous entities and situations. Five ranks, lowest to highest: Janitor, Rookie, Operator, Handler, Director. Operators are assigned bodyguards in the field (Korval is Osyra's). Codenames are used during formal events—mission radio chatter, official briefings, filed reports—and dropped for real names otherwise.

**Ranks**
- **Janitor** — separate teams that show up after Headquarters decides the Operators are done. They clean up what's left at the site. When Janitors arrive, the work is over.
- **Rookie** — Operator in training. Field-track but not yet trusted with their own case.
- **Operator** — full field rank. Hunts down anomalies, retrieves artifacts, handles situations. Osyra is an Operator.
- **Handler** — manages Operators from headquarters. Runs the desk, keeps the records, makes the calls. Each Operator has a Handler; some Handlers run more than one Operator. Vanity is a Handler, nicknamed Wiki because she keeps the record on everything.
- **Director** — top of the agency.

---

### Anomalous Entities

**Overview**
Otherworldly creatures, objects that warp reality, things that bend the mind. They don't follow normal rules. Some ignore Chimerans entirely. Some respond to them specifically.

---

### The World

**Overview**
A modern setting. Cities with metropolis centers that give way to rural neighborhoods. Hotels, cars, Polaroid cameras. A world that looks normal on the surface but has things hiding underneath it.

"Hey. My name is Osyra. This is my video application."
I held up the piece of paper. It bleached the screen - a stark white rectangle in the dark of my

it while setting up and left it. Let the light do what it wants. My hood was up. Between the hood

"I'm told someone put my name forward," I said. "That you already know the broad strokes, and
you'd rather hear the rest from me. Fine."
"My parents had nothing-I was one mouth too many. They kept me anyway until I was six, and

care, one house after another. Until I was sixteen. Then the man who was supposed to be keeping
me sold me to a facility."
I stopped there. Longer than I meant to. I'd written that sentence down and said it out loud maybe
forty times in the two hours before I hit record, sanding it flat, taking the feeling out of it word by
word until it was just a series of events that had happened to a person. It still cost something to
say into a lens. I looked down, and my hair swept past my glasses, and I put my eyes back into the
camera and made my face do nothing.
"They ran illegal experiments. Hybridization-splicing animal into human and seeing what lived. I
was a line item. A body nobody who mattered would come looking for."
"They had charts of my development, my reflexes, my cellular response. Most of the others didn't
survive what they were given. I did. When the place burned down I walked out, and I lived like an
animal for the better part of two years-started in the woods, ended up on the street-taking what I
needed, answering to no one. Long enough to learn I could."
I took a breath and held it a second before I let it go.
Here was the problem I'd been working the whole time, and I want to be honest that I never
solved it, I only ran out of night. They already knew. Somebody in the smoking circle had put my
name in and that name came with the rest of it attached, so the ears were not information. They
were theater. The only question on the table was who got to do the reveal - them, in a file, in an

hood.

## Chapter 2 - The Verification

kept tuning to the wrong things - the drip of a tap, something scuttling in the walls, the low thrum
of a city that never stops. When I did drift, it wasn't dreams. It was a replay of sensations: the cold
press of a stethoscope, the bite of a needle, a scientist's voice noting observations in a flat,
delighted tone.
That's what the delight does. It stays. A man can hurt you and the hurt will fade on schedule, but a
man who is pleased while he does it puts something in the recording that doesn't come out.

The window opened. Just me in a small square, and a larger, empty black one labeled Dr. Vasquez

It felt absurd. Like a warm-up for a workout video. I did it - rolled them forward, feeling the

I pulled my lips back. A snarl I didn't feel. The points caught the light.
A pause. More typing.

Two o'clock. Today. No time to think. No time to back out.
Which was the design. I'd worked that out by then. You don't schedule a person five hours out

Baseline data. There is a filing cabinet somewhere with my baseline data in it, taken when I was

Whatever else the lab wanted, it wanted this, and it wanted it in a way that came off the page.
She started standard - heart rate, palpation of the abdomen. Her hands were professional. When
she pressed near my ovaries a jolt went through me, deep, resonant, and she paused, gaze
sharpening. "Interesting."
The internal ultrasound was next. Cold gel, intrusive probe. I stared at a crack in the ceiling tile.
On the screen, grey shapes swirled. My own insides, looking alien.
"Uterine structure appears normal," she said quietly. "Ovarian activity is... significant. Follicular
development is advanced."
She removed the probe and sat on a stool, facing me. Expression neutral, open. "Osyra, I need you
to describe your reproductive cycle for me. In your own words. What do you experience?"
The question was a blade.
I had never said it out loud. Not once, to anyone, in two years of living with it. I had said nothing
about it to anyone at all, ever, and here was a stranger with a tablet asking me to narrate it in a
cold room with gel drying on my stomach, and the terrible part - the part I have never gotten past
- is that she was the first person to ask. The lab measured it. She asked me what it was like.
"It's not a period," I started, the distinction clawing out of me. "It runs on a different clock. Once a
year, in the fall - same stretch every time. I can mark it on a calendar. And it comes in stages."
"Walk me through them."
I kept my voice flat. Read it off like a report. "First I can't settle. Don't sleep. Everything gets too
loud, too bright, too much - my own skin feels like it's listening. I get short with people. There's
some bleeding, early on, a few days of it." A muscle moved in my jaw. "Then it sharpens into a
pull. Toward-" I stopped. Made myself finish. "Toward closeness. Contact. For a few days I want it
badly enough that I don't trust myself with it. Then it breaks and I crash - wrung out for a week.
After that it's gone for another year."
She nodded, typing. "A seasonal estrous cycle. Photoperiod-triggered - your body reads the
shortening daylight and runs the program on schedule. Textbook canid, grafted onto a human
frame. The serum rewired your hypothalamic-pituitary axis." She leaned forward slightly.
"Predictable, at least. That helps. But that drive window is a profound liability in the field if it isn't
managed."
She wasn't judging. That's what she was doing instead: assessing a flawed asset.
"Given Titan Reach's operational parameters, we will provide a regimen of hormonal regulators.
Non-negotiable for field readiness. The prescription and a monitoring kit will be couriered to
you."
A chemical leash for the biological one.
And I sat there and turned it over and could not find the objection. That's what I want on the
record. It is a liability. She's right. There are three days a year I would not trust myself with a
weapon and a door, and I have known that since the first fall, and I have never had a solution for
it, and a stranger solved it in ninety seconds with a courier.
The help and the leash were the same object. There was no version where I got one without the
other. There has never been a version where I got one without the other - that's the whole
architecture of my life, and Titan Reach didn't build it, they just read the schematic and priced it
out.
"Understood," I said. The word was ash.
She stood. "Your body is operating on their schedule, Osyra. We're just helping you keep time."
A professional nod, and she left. I don't think she knew what she'd said. I think she meant it
kindly.
Vasquez came back in, looking from her clipboard to me. The clinical detachment had hardened
into something else. Verification. Conquest. "The Chimera 9 expression is valid and fully
integrated. You have passed the final interview."
I dressed in the empty alcove, yanking my hoodie on, hood up the second it cleared my head. The
clinic's smell was in my nose and on my skin. No relief. Just a hollow echo where relief should
have been.
Passed.
I'd graduated from the lab to a bigger, better-funded cage. The tests were over. Somebody had
looked at every part of me and written valid next to it.
Somehow that was the worst possible outcome, and it took me a long time to be able to say why:
because I wanted it. Because when she said you have passed something in me sat up. Twelve years
of being a line item - foster paperwork, then lab charts - and I still went warm at a stranger telling
me I'd measured up.
They didn't have to break me. They just had to grade me.

## Chapter 4 - The Rooftop
A week passed. The couriered package arrived: a blister pack of pills and a clinical-grade basal
thermometer.
I left it on the kitchen counter. Untouched. A tiny rebellion, and I knew exactly how tiny while I
was having it. Every itch, every sharpened smell, I monitored with a dread that curdled into
something like defiance.
This is still mine.
That's the whole argument. That's all I had. It isn't much of one - I was defending my ownership of
a thing that was installed in me by men I never consented to meet, which makes it about as mine
as a bullet is mine once it's in me. But there's a difference between a thing being done to you and a
thing you decide to carry, and the difference is the only property I've ever actually held title to.
The drive window came two weeks after the exam, right on the calendar I've never needed to write
down.
Not a surprise. An ambush I chose.
The restlessness I'd carried for days sharpened, by dusk, into something with claws. A wire pulled
taut. My apartment turned into a torture chamber of amplified sensation - the fridge motor a
jackhammer, the smell of my own skin sour and overwhelming. The pills gleamed on the counter.
Taking them felt like letting them win. Not taking them felt like insanity. I want to note that I
could see, even then, that "letting them win" is not a medical consideration. I could see it and it
did not move me one inch.
I couldn't be contained. I went to the only place left. The roof.
* * *
The night air was cool on skin that ran too hot and too aware. The city sprawled below, a map of
smells and distant sounds. I paced the gravel, tail lashing, ears swiveling at every siren, every
shout off the streets. The need was a physical pressure, a hollow pulling throb.
That was when I caught his scent.
It cut through the chemical haze of the city - clean soap, fresh linen, and under it something warm
and vital. Human. But distinct. Compelling.
My head snapped up.
He was at the far end of the roof, leaning on the parapet, looking at the skyline. A silhouette.
I was across the roof without deciding to go. My body moved and my mind came along behind it,
and I'd like to state plainly that this is the thing I fear most in the world and I did it in under four
seconds for a man I could smell.
He turned as I came. Tall. Kind eyes that widened in surprise, then went to careful curiosity as he
took me in - the hood, the ears in the moonlight, the agitated tail.
Most people run. Or scream. He watched.
His eyes went from my pinned ears to my too-bright gaze, and his expression shifted from
curiosity to a dawning, careful comprehension. He didn't look at my ears like they were costumes.
He looked at them like they were symptoms.
"You're... in some kind of trouble," he said softly. Not a question about what I was. A question
about the state I was in.
"You shouldn't be up here alone," I said, and it came out a rough scrape. It wasn't a warning. It
was a confession.
"Neither should you." Calm. A deep, steady sound that vibrated in my chest. "I'm Leo. I live
downstairs."
I stopped a few feet off, close enough that his scent washed over me. The animal screamed to press
closer, to rub my cheek against his jacket, to taste the salt on his skin.
I locked my knees to stay still.
"Osyra," I managed.
He nodded, gaze dropping to my twitching ears, then back to my eyes. He didn't flinch from the
red. He glanced from my face to the city far below, and something in his expression settled with
decision, not fear.
"What do you need?" he asked.
The question was simple and it took me apart.
I didn't have an answer that wasn't primal. Contact. Pressure. You.
So I reached out instead. Slowly. Giving him every chance to pull back. My fingertips brushed the
back of his hand where it rested on the ledge.
The contact was electric. A jolt that straightened my spine. His skin was cool. My whole world
narrowed to that point.
He turned his hand over. Palm up. An invitation, or a surrender.
I slid my fingers into his. His grip was firm. Steadying.
And the floodgate opened - all the frantic scattered energy in me coalesced and ran toward that
anchor. The painful edge of it blunted. Not gone. Channeled.
We stood there in the dark, hand in hand, not speaking. The city noise went to a murmur. My
breathing slowed to match his. My ears, which had been flicking at everything, relaxed and rotated
gently toward the rhythm of his heartbeat. My tail stilled and curled around my own ankle.
It wasn't passion. Not yet. It was something more fundamental. A quieting. A recognition.
The thing in my blood, frantic and howling, saw him and settled.
He lifted our joined hands, thumb stroking a slow line across my knuckles. Unbearably gentle.
Human.
"Is this okay?" he whispered.
I leaned my forehead against his shoulder and hid my face in the clean linen smell of his shirt. I
nodded.
For the first time since it started, the cage wasn't pressing in. The door wasn't open, but the lock
felt less heavy.
He held my hand in the dark, and for a little while I wasn't a subject, or an applicant, or a monster.
I was a girl on a rooftop holding onto something warm, waiting for the heat to break.
* * *
I went inside.
The ghost of his touch felt different under a ceiling. Not like a connection. Like a smudge.
The pill kit sat on the counter. A stark, clinical answer to the mess I'd just made. Non-negotiable
for field readiness. What a joke. I couldn't manage a hand on a roof.
I punched a pill through the foil.
A small white admission of failure. I'd gone up there to escape the frenzy and what had I done -
dragged a stranger into it. Practically glued myself to some poor guy trying to get some air. He'd
been kind, which made it worse. He saw a woman in obvious distress and he was decent about it,
and he had no idea the distress was an engineered biological command, that I'd honed in on his
scent like a predator picks a line.
My skin crawled with it.
The pill wasn't about control going forward. It was an eraser for tonight.
I swallowed it dry. The bitter chalk was a fitting punishment.
* * *
That night the heat didn't vanish, but its meaning changed. The need was still a thrum in my
blood; the frantic compulsive story around it was gone. And the memory of leaning on him, of
twining my fingers with his, replayed in my head not as solace but as a cringing lapse in judgment.
What was I thinking?
I hadn't been. That was the whole problem.
I fell asleep wrapped in a cold blanket of regret, the arousal reduced to a dull physiological echo,
stripped of its dangerous illusion.
* * *
Morning brought a brutal clarity. The fog was gone, burned off by the regulator. With it went the
last of the heat's romantic lie.
In the crisp quiet light I saw the rooftop for what it was: a vulnerable, potentially catastrophic
mistake. Leo wasn't a point of quiet humanity. He was a variable. An unknown. A random man
who now had a very strange story to tell, if he chose to.
My ears flattened as I replayed it. The neediness. The trembling. Offering my name like a gift,
when it was just another piece of data I'd handed over for free.
A cold sharp peace cut through me. This was the clarity I needed. No more messing with
strangers. No more letting the animal dictate my interactions. The pill gave me the silence to hear
my own priorities and they were singular: survive, get control, get out. Anything else was a
liability.
I've had a long time to look at that morning, and I want to set down what I've since worked out
about it, because I couldn't see it then and it was the whole thing.
Consider what the pill actually did.
It did not make me see the night correctly. It made me see the night coldly, and I have spent my
entire life treating those as the same word. Cold is my instrument. Cold is how I know something's
true. So when I woke up and the memory had gone from a quieting to a lapse, I didn't experience
that as a chemical rewriting my testimony. I experienced it as waking up.
But the man on the roof asked me what I needed and then gave it to me and did not ask for one
thing back. That happened. It happened whether or not there was a drug in my blood when I
remembered it. The pill did not add any information. It only changed the verdict, and it changed
the verdict in exactly the direction the company that couriered it would prefer - toward variable,
toward liability, toward a closed system that doesn't reach for anybody and can be deployed
anywhere.
They didn't have to tell me to think that. They just had to make thinking it feel like sobriety.
I never took a second one. I want that on the record, because it matters to what comes after and
because I have never told anyone.
One pill. That's all it took. The pack sat on that counter for two weeks and I didn't touch it again - I
didn't need to. The heat broke on its own schedule, the way it always does, and by then the thing
the pill had done to my memory of the roof was permanent. You don't have to keep taking
something that only had to work once.
The placid, emotionless days that followed were a relief, and they were mine. No chemistry in
them. That's the part I couldn't see: I'd swallowed one tablet as an eraser and then spent a
fortnight doing the erasing myself, for free, and calling it clarity.
Then the phone rang.

## Chapter 5 - The Offer
The manufactured quiet was broken by a single phone call. It didn't come from a blocked number.
It came with a title.
"This is Lieutenant Colonel Daniel Thorne, Joint Special Operations Personnel Division." A voice
carved out of granite, with the ghost of a southern drawl smoothing the edges. No warmth in it.
Only authority. "I am contacting you on behalf of our private-sector liaison, Titan Reach
Consulting."
My grip tightened on the phone. I was sitting on the fire escape, which was two floors and one
week from the rooftop and felt like another country. My tail lay inert.
"They've forwarded your clinical evaluation package, Ms. Osyra. It's... thorough. Dr. Vasquez's
notes indicate you possess a... unique aptitude profile."
I heard the subtext. A controlled asset. Or a loose cannon.
Both of those are things you buy. Nobody makes a phone call about a third option.
"Titan Reach has a proposal. They field operatives. You've passed the medical. Now you face the
practical."
The sun felt interrogative.
"They have a dedicated training curriculum at a secure facility. A final evaluation under simulated
high-stress conditions. Think of it as a combine for people who hunt things that shouldn't exist. If
you pass, a formal job offer is guaranteed. Full benefits, structured pay, a defined role. A purpose."
A purpose. A structure. Walls to replace the cage. Rules to prevent rooftop mistakes.
"And if I fail?" Flat.
A pause. "Failure in this line of work has permanent consequences. But in the context of the
evaluation, it means you walk away. No offer. Your file is closed. Titan Reach considers the matter
concluded."
Concluded. Either they'd forget me, or they'd make certain I stopped being a variable. He left the
gap on purpose and let me furnish it myself, which is cheaper than a threat and does the same
work.
"What does it involve?"
"Need-to-know, delivered on-site. It will assess tactical acumen, threat response, team
interoperability, and stress resilience under controlled exposures. All necessary equipment
provided. You need only bring yourself."
And the discipline not to compromise yourself. A stranger's hand, sharp in my mind.
"You have forty-eight hours to decide. An NDA will be transmitted. Sign it, and coordinates for
transport will follow. Do not sign it, and this conversation never happened."
The line died.
* * *
I sat in the fading light and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was clean, and I want to walk
through it exactly as I had it then, because I have never been able to find the error and I have
looked.
The pills on my counter were proof I couldn't trust my own biology.
The rooftop was proof I couldn't trust my own judgment once the biology had hold of it.
That's two of the three legs gone. You cannot stand on one. So the only move left was to stop
trying to be my own authority and hand the job to something bigger with clearer rules - outsource
the trust. Titan Reach doesn't care what I regret. It cares what I can do. There's a kind of mercy in
a system that only grades your output, and I had been looking for that mercy my whole life
without a name for it.
The recruiter's call was a lifeline thrown off the same ship that designed my storm. It was the only
play on the board.
Here's what I couldn't see, sitting on that fire escape with the phone going cold in my hand.
Every piece of evidence in that proof was manufactured by the people making the offer. The
clinical established the liability. The courier delivered the fix. The fix wrote the verdict on the roof.
And forty-eight hours later a colonel called to tell me the solution to my unreliability was to
become theirs.
I had one data point that didn't come from them - a man asking what I needed and then just
giving it, on a roof, for free, at no profit to anyone. That one point disproved the whole model. I
had already thrown it out. I'd thrown it out the morning after, in a bathroom, and called the
throwing-out clarity.
I went inside. The NDA glowed on the screen.
I didn't hesitate. The shame of what I'd been with a stranger was a sharper spur than any
ambition. It moved me faster than money would have. That's worth noticing, if anyone's keeping
score: they didn't have to offer me enough. They only had to make me ashamed of the one hour of
my life that hadn't cost anybody anything.
I typed my name.
The screen refreshed. Coordinates. A time: 04:30. Pack Light.
I looked at the pills on the counter. Two weeks they'd sat there. I hadn't needed a second one and I
wasn't going to - whatever that tablet was for, it had done its work in a single night and the work
had held.
I left them where they were and went to find a bag.
The only thing I really needed to pack was the resolve not to be a mistake again.

## Chapter 6 - The Combine
Transport was a black SUV with tinted windows and a driver who didn't speak. It felt less like a
ride and more like an extradition.
The coordinates led to a private airstrip where a twin-prop, unmarked and utilitarian, swallowed
me and two other recruits - a wiry man who kept cracking his knuckles and a woman with a
thousand-yard stare who looked at nothing at all. We flew in silence, the drone of the engines a
physical pressure in my ears.
We landed on a dusty strip carved into dense evergreen forest. The air was thin and cold and
smelled of pine resin and damp earth. A concrete bunker squatted at the tree line. A sign over the
heavy double doors: TITAN REACH - ASSESSMENT & ACCLIMATIZATION FACILITY.
Inside was harsh fluorescence and echoing linoleum. A bored sergeant at a kiosk took my name,
scanned my retina, slapped a plastic wristband on me. "Welcome to the Combine, cadet. Follow
the green line. Don't wander. Don't touch anything."
The green line was a literal strip of luminous tape on the floor. It led through a maze of corridors.
The place hummed - sterile, purposeful. Clatter of gear. Murmur of voices behind closed doors.
The distant thump of something heavy being dropped on a mat.
And everywhere, the stares.
The first cluster saw me as I was funneled into a holding area - a gymnasium converted into a
processing hub, rows of temporary cots and locker bays. Maybe two dozen people in various stages
of intake, all hard-looking, all sizing each other up.
My hood was up. My tail was visible. The tips of my ears came through my hair.
The murmurs started like a rustle of dry leaves.
"...the hell is that?"
"Check out the tail. No way."
"Costume party's that way, freak."
A big guy with a shaved head and a neck like a tree stump nudged his friend, a lanky redhead.
"Look, a Fido. You lose your master, puppy?"
The redhead grinned. "Maybe she's here for the K-9 unit. Wrong door, Furry."
Heat came up my neck. The old kind. The shameful kind, not the biological one.
They're different. I'd never had cause to notice before, and I noticed then, standing on a green line
with a duffel: the thing in the fall that makes me want is machinery. It's chimeran. It runs on
daylight and it has a courier and a dosage.
This isn't that. This is older than the serum. Nobody engineered the part of me that goes hot when
a man calls me a dog - that was installed the ordinary way, by people, in rooms, over years, and
there is no tablet for it and there never will be.
I kept my eyes on the green line and my face blank. Don't react. Don't give them the satisfaction.
Same rule as every lab, every school hallway, every crowded street. I've been running that rule so
long it isn't a decision anymore, it's a reflex, and the tragedy of a good reflex is you stop noticing
what it costs to fire.
A staff member in grey fatigues appeared with a clipboard. "Osyra. With me. Custom fitting."
I broke from the line and felt every eye bore into my back as I followed him out a side door.
That became the rhythm of the day. Brief periods in the common areas, punctuated by constant
solitary summons.
I understood the pattern by the third one and I have never decided whether it was designed. Every
time the room started to get used to me, they took me out of it. Every time I came back I was new
again, and a new thing in a room full of frightened people is a target. If someone drew that up on a
whiteboard, it's the most elegant cruelty I've ever been on the receiving end of. If nobody did - if
it's just what happens when a bureaucracy processes an exception - that's worse, because then
there's nobody to be angry at.
The gear fitting was in a small armory. A taciturn technician measured me with laser calipers,
brow furrowed. "Cranial circumference accounting for auricular structures... cervical spine to
lumbar, note the caudal integration point... we'll need a custom helmet, modified body armor for
range of motion and tail articulation." He muttered into a headset. "Yeah, send up the prototyping
putty. It's the chimera candidate."
It's the chimera candidate. Not she's here. Not even a name. I was standing close enough to touch
him.
Back in the gym, a new group had arrived. The whispers were louder now. More confident.
"Ugly as sin. What kind of sick mod is that?"
"Bet it smells like wet dog."
I found a vacant cot in a corner and sat with my knees pulled up, making myself small. Six foot
four and making myself small. The stares had weight.
Then a different summons. "Osyra. Medical Bay 3. Vaccine protocol."
In a stark white room a doctor with a nervous smile consulted a thick digital file. "Ah, yes. Subject
presents with a recombinant retro-viral integration. Standard immunoboosters are
contraindicated. We'll be administering a tailored series to avoid triggering an autoimmune
response against the non-human cellular lines."
The non-human cellular lines. Mine. He meant mine. He said it to the file.
The shots burned - a deep aching cold spreading out from the sites.
Each time I came back to the gym the hostility had crystallized a degree further. I wasn't a
curiosity anymore. I was a target, and I understood exactly what for, because it's the same
everywhere and I've watched it work my whole life.
Two dozen people in that room were terrified. Every one of them had signed something they didn't
fully read to come to a bunker in a forest to be tested at something they might fail. That fear has to
go somewhere. It cannot go up the chain - the chain is what they're afraid of. So it goes sideways,
and it lands on whatever's furthest from the middle, and in that gymnasium the furthest thing
from the middle was six foot four with ears.
I was doing them a service. That's what sits badly. Every joke about the dog was a man moving his
fear off his own back and onto mine, and it worked - I could see it working, I could see the
shoulders come down after - and none of them will ever know they used me for it.
"Hey, Mutant," the big one - Stumps - called as I came back from medical with my arm throbbing.
"They giving you your rabies shot? Should be mandatory."
A few chuckles rippled around him.
I kept walking. Tail rigid. Ears flat inside my hood.

## Chapter 7 - The Prototype
That was when a new voice cut through. Quiet, but carrying a strange deliberate intensity.
"Shut up, Evers."
It came from a recruit leaning against the lockers. Tall, leanly built, sharp watchful features, dark
hair cropped close. His eyes - a pale crystalline grey - were fixed not on me but on Stumps. No
anger in his face. Just cool assessment.
Stumps puffed up. "What'd you say, pretty boy?"
The lean one pushed off the lockers. He didn't move aggressively. He moved with a relaxed
economy that was somehow worse. He glanced at me, gaze sweeping from my hidden ears to my
posture and back to Stumps, and his eyes widened a fraction, like he was connecting dots only he
could see.
"I said, shut up. You have no idea what you're looking at."
"I'm looking at a circus act," Stumps sneered.
The lean one shook his head slowly, a faint almost awed smile touching his mouth. He looked back
at me, and this time the stare wasn't disgust or mockery.
It was fascination. Unvarnished. Awe.
"No," he said, loud enough for the others. A hush fell, eager for new drama. "You're looking at a
prototype. I've heard whispers about this. Deep-bio black ops stuff. They don't mod for looks.
They mod for function."
He took a step closer, eyes locked on me like I was a fascinating blueprint.
"Look at the base of the spine. Perfect for balance and kinetic transfer. The auricular mobility?
360-degree audio surveillance, no tech required. The ocular pigmentation? That's for low-light
and thermal tracking."
He was making it up. I could hear him making it up - the cadence of a man assembling a theory
out loud and falling in love with it as it comes. His conviction was magnetic and there was not one
sourced fact in it.
He was also right. That's the joke. Everything he invented, they did on purpose. Not for the
reasons he said and not for the war he imagined, but a room full of men did sit down and decide
what my spine was going to be for. He got the answer right off a wrong proof, which is the most
common way anyone has ever been right about me.
"They didn't make a pet. They built a super-soldier. And you're calling it names."
The silence in our corner was absolute. Stumps's bluster deflated into a dawning uncertain fear.
The others stared at me with new eyes - not a monster now. A weapon. The mockery gone,
replaced by wary calculating distance.
The lean one held my gaze a moment longer.
No pity in it. No kindness either. Just the cool approving gleam of a fanatic recognizing a superior
piece of engineering. He gave me a slight, almost imperceptible nod.
Then he turned and walked away, leaving a void of tense quiet behind him.
* * *
I sat back down on my cot with his words going around in my head. Super-soldier. Prototype.
Built.
He hadn't defended me. I want to be precise, because for about four seconds I thought he had, and
those four seconds were embarrassing. He didn't say she's a person, leave her alone. He said
you're too stupid to see what that is. He moved me from one shelf to another. Both shelves are in
a display case.
But the stares had changed, and that was worth something. Not mocking now. Wary. Calculating.
Stumps wouldn't meet my eyes at all.
Fear is a cleaner barrier than contempt. It's simpler. It gives you room.
I pulled my hood tighter. And somewhere under the flat surface I keep over everything, something
pragmatic came together.
Here's the trade, laid out honestly, the way I laid it out then.
They will never let me be a person in this building. That option is not on the table and I did not
put it there and I cannot put it there. What's on the table is pet or weapon. Those are the two
shelves. Fido, or prototype.
Pet costs me everything and buys nothing. Weapon costs me the same thing - it costs being seen, it
costs any chance that one of these two dozen people ever looks at me and thinks her - and it buys
distance. Distance is the only currency in a room like that. I have spent my life shopping in stores
that only carry two items and I have gotten very good at knowing which one is cheaper.
So: let them have the weapon. Lean into the rumor. Wear their awed fear like a cloak. They want
to see a thing that was built? Fine. It's better than being a pet, a freak, a Fido. Inside these walls,
performance is everything. I could get respect out of this, or at least a safe radius, by pretending to
be exactly the monster they'd already decided I was.
It's a good trade. It's the best available trade. I made it in about thirty seconds on a cot in a
gymnasium and I'd make it again.
I noticed, distantly, the way you notice weather through a window, that I was agreeing to be the
thing the lab built. That the men who put their hands on me at sixteen would have found this
outcome entirely satisfactory. That somewhere there's a file with my name on it and a column
marked disposition, and I had just, voluntarily, at no cost to anybody, filled it in.
I noticed it and nothing moved. That's mine. Nobody drugged me into that. I have been doing it to
myself for years and I am better at it than any chemist.
Boot camp hadn't officially started, and I was already alone in a room full of people. But now, at
least, I had a role to play.

## Chapter 8 - Shark Attack
The tension in the gymnasium settled into a fragile watchful stalemate over the week. The
mockery died, choked off by the super-soldier rumor and by my own deliberate silence.
I perfected the stare. A flat unblinking look that makes red eyes read less like a flaw and more like
a targeting laser. I'd aim it at anyone who lingered and they'd find something else to look at.
It works. That's what I'd underestimated. I'd spent my whole life trying to be smaller and it turns
out the whole time there was a lever right there marked be worse and it works instantly and costs
nothing up front.
Processing was a blur of custom fittings, psych evals, endless waiting. On the second day they
issued the uniform: standard Titan Reach fatigues, digital grey-green, but mine had been subtly
altered. Collar widened. Trousers reinforced with a gusset of tougher material at the base of the
spine.
Wearing it felt strange. Less like clothes. More like being fitted for a new and official kind of skin.
The message was clear, and it was two messages at once, which I think is the most honest thing
that building ever said to me: you are standardized and you are apart. Same garment. They had
to build a special version of the uniform that makes everyone the same.
The helmet came the same day, in its own hard case, because it couldn't go in a duffel with the
standard kit - wrong shape, wrong shell, two channels moulded into the crown that no other
helmet on the manifest has. The technician handed it to me like it was ordinary.
Everyone else's helmet went in the bag. Mine came with luggage.
* * *
The day of transport to the main barracks, the distinction went public.
Pre-dawn formation in the yard outside the bunker. Fifty of us, a ragged shivering line in identical
fatigues, clutching duffels of standard-issue gear. No discipline yet. Just a jostling anxious crowd.
A staff sergeant with a face like a hatchet stood on a crate. "Listen up, maggots! You are no longer
civilians. You are names on a manifest. You will be addressed by your last names, and you will
respond with 'Yes, Sergeant!' or 'No, Sergeant!' Is that understood?"
A mumbled uneven chorus came back.
"Pathetic! Now, first row, move! And keep your traps shut!"
As the first recruits shuffled toward the idling bus, his eyes found me at the back. He pointed a
rigid finger.
"You. Renaya. The special project. You're separate. You do not board until I say. You will take the
last seat, the one marked with red tape. You will not speak to anyone. You follow all instructions
given to the group, but you do it alone. You are not part of this platoon. You are an attachment. Do
you comprehend?"
Every head in uniform turned.
Renaya. He used my name. It was the first time in that building anybody had, and he used it to
explain to fifty people that I wasn't one of them.
"Yes, Sergeant," I said. Clear and toneless.
"Good. Remember it."
One by one the last names were barked and recruits filed on. I watched them go with my duffel at
my feet. Finally: "Renaya! Forward!"
I walked the gauntlet of faces in the bus windows. Curious. Hostile. Afraid. I climbed the steps.
The interior was silent, the air thick.
And there at the very back was a single seat, its cracked vinyl marked with a glaring X of red duct
tape.
An island. Somebody had gone out there with a roll of tape, before dawn, and marked it. That's a
work order. Somewhere there's a line item for the tape.
"Sit!" the sergeant yelled from outside.
I slid in and stowed my gear. The space around me felt electrically charged. A buffer zone of pure
isolation. The driver closed the door and the bus lurched forward, winding deeper into the forest.
* * *
For the first hour, tense exhausted quiet.
Then, thirty minutes out, it began.
A single brutal shout from the front. A drill instructor I hadn't seen board was suddenly on his
feet, face contorted with fury.
"WHAT IS THIS, A GIRL SCOUT TOUR? THIS BUS IS NOW A WARZONE! HEADS! BETWEEN!
YOUR! KNEES! NOW! ALL OF YOU! DO IT!"
Chaos. Recruits fumbled, diving forward, caps tumbling. "I CAN'T HEAR YOUR FOREHEADS
HITTING THE SEATS! HARDER! YOU THINK THE ENEMY GIVES YOU NECK PILLOWS?"
The Shark Attack. The deliberate overwhelming verbal assault built to erase individuality and
install instant blind obedience. He paced the aisle, voice a constant deafening percussion against
the metal shell.
"NO LOOKING! EYES ON YOUR BOOTS OR THE DECK! YOU LOOK AT ME, I'LL
PERSONALLY PEEL YOUR EYELIDS BACK! SHUT UP! THE ONLY SOUND I WANT IS YOUR
WEAK LITTLE HEARTS TRYING NOT TO GIVE OUT!"
I followed the order, forehead against the hard shell of the helmet case in my hands, the stiff brim
of my cap digging into my wrist. My ears, pinned flat under it, rang with the volume. Every shout a
physical slap.
He did not single me out.
His boots crunched past the red-taped seat without pause. I was just another recruit in fatigues
with my head down, obeying.
"YOU ARE NOTHING! YOU ARE WORTHLESS! YOU WILL DO WHAT YOU ARE TOLD, WHEN
YOU ARE TOLD, HOW YOU ARE TOLD! YOUR GEAR IS YOUR ONLY FRIEND! GET IT
BETWEEN YOUR LEGS! HUG IT! LOVE IT! IT'S THE ONLY THING THAT MIGHT SAVE YOUR
MISERABLE LIFE!"
The bus echoed with scrambling, with duffels wrestled into position, with panicked held breaths.
And I want to put down what happened in me during that half hour, because I have not been able
to explain it to anybody since and I am not sure it reflects well on me.
It was the best I had felt in two years.
You are nothing. He screamed it at fifty people and he meant every one of them and he did not
look at me when he said it. Nobody in that bus was a chimera candidate. Nobody was an auricular
structure or a caudal integration point or a non-human cellular line. We were all worthless,
identically, at the top of a man's lungs, and for thirty minutes I was not the exception to anything.
That's all it took. That's the entire price of admission to the human race, as far as I can tell: find
someone who hates everyone equally.
I hugged a duffel between my knees with my forehead on a helmet case and my ears bleeding
sound, and I was so grateful I could have laughed.
The Shark Attack ran the whole final half hour. By the time the bus crunched onto gravel, the
silence that replaced the shouting was hollow and ringing. My neck ached. My hands were numb
from gripping the case.
"OFF MY BUS! NOW! MOVE, MOVE, MOVE! FORM A LINE ON THE YARD! DUFFELS AT
YOUR FEET! EYES FRONT! MOVE LIKE YOUR LIVES DEPEND ON IT, BECAUSE STARTING
TODAY, THEY DO!"
Recruits stumbled into the aisle, uniforms rumpled, movements jerky with residual fear.
I waited, as instructed, until the bus was nearly empty. Then I stood, adjusted my cap, slung my
duffel, took up the case.
Last one down the steps.
* * *
The yard was shell-shocked recruits trying to form lines under the blistering shouts of three drill
instructors. One of them - a mountain of a man with a shaved scalp and the same voice that had
run the bus - saw me. His eyes took in the uniform, the case, my position. No personal malice in
them. Just the flat assessing glare he'd give any piece of irregular equipment on his manifest.
"RENAYA!" he bellowed. Not a taunt. A designation. "The attachment! You don't get in line! You
stand there!" He pointed to a spot ten feet left of the ragged formation, the gesture as precise as a
surveyor's. "You are a shadow! You follow all training, but you are not of this platoon!
Understood?"
"Understood, Drill Sergeant!" I shouted. Raw in my throat.
I marched to the spot, dropped my duffel, snapped to attention, the case rigid at my side.
Thirty minutes. That's how long it lasted. Off the bus and back onto my own square of gravel, ten
feet from everybody, measured out by a man with a pointing finger.
He gave me one final impersonal glance - a commander confirming a logistical detail - then turned
his full terrifying focus back to the platoon. "WHAT ARE YOU STARING AT, MAGGOTS? YOUR
JOB IS TO LEARN, NOT GAPE! EYES FRONT!"
I stared straight ahead, over the heads of the identically dressed recruits, at the stark grey wall of
the nearest barracks.
The case bit into my palm through the glove. The Shark Attack's adrenaline was still going in my
veins with nowhere to put itself.
Ten feet. It's not far. You could hand somebody a cup of coffee across ten feet.

## Chapter 9 - Broken Down
The first week wasn't about being different. It was about being broken down.
The humiliation was total and it was democratic. It wasn't personal, because nothing there was
personal. You were a body in uniform and your body was weak, slow, and stupid. One recruit
fumbled a drill step and the whole platoon paid in push-ups on the gravel, the DI's voice
shredding the air above us. Somebody's footlocker sat a millimeter out of alignment at inspection
and we all stood at attention for an hour in the biting cold while he methodically dumped every
locker in the bay onto the floor.
The lesson was a blunt instrument: you are a cell in an organism. One cell fails, the whole body
suffers.
I understood the architecture of it within about two days, and I want to be fair to them - it's good
work. It is the most efficient machine for making a group out of strangers that I have ever been
inside of. You don't build loyalty by asking for it. You build it by charging everyone for one
person's mistake until the arithmetic makes them each other's problem.
I could see it working. I watched it work on fifty people.
It did not work on me, and not because I resisted it. Because you can't be made into a cell of an
organism from ten feet outside the organism.
My status as the attachment meant I stood apart during formations but suffered alongside them
for the consequences. I did the push-ups. I stood in the cold. I scrubbed. And I did all of it a full
head taller than almost everyone.
At six-four my height had always been a fact. In ranks of recruits averaging five-ten it became a
permanent feature of the landscape. I had to consciously slump during some drills to not break
the line - a habit the DIs beat out of me fast.
"STAND AT YOUR GOD-GIVEN ATTENTION, RENAYA! DON'T APOLOGIZE FOR THE
BLUEPRINT!"
God-given. That's the joke, and he'll never know he made it.
There was so much cleaning. Floors had to gleam like obsidian. Latrines had to smell of nothing
but bleach. We scrubbed with toothbrushes, with rags, with our own fingernails. Industrial cleaner
became a permanent note in the back of my throat. My height was an advantage there - no shelf
out of reach, so I got the high dusting and the light fixtures while the others grumbled at
baseboards.
Physical training was a relentless grind. Calisthenics at 0400 in the dark. Long grueling runs in
formation, the pack's rhythm a punishing collective heartbeat. My physiology handled the cardio
better than most - my lungs burned but they didn't scream - and my legs gave me a stride that
made the runs feel almost natural, though I had to check my pace constantly to keep from
overtaking the file.
Constantly. Every run. That's the detail I'd give anyone who asked what that month was actually
like: I spent it running slower than I can run, so that a line of men could stay in front of me.
The volume of it wore on everyone. My muscles ached with a new deep fatigue and my tail often
felt like dead weight, a clumsy appendage I had to consciously tuck and control.
By the third day a plague of respiratory illness swept the bay. Wet hacking coughs and congested
sniffling, a constant chorus. Sinus pain made faces puffy and eyes watery. The DIs showed no
mercy. "I DON'T CARE IF YOU'RE COUGHING UP A LUNG, MAGGOT! YOU MOVE!"
I watched recruits stumble through drills shivering with fever and collapse afterward.
I stayed healthy. Another side-effect - the lab's logs called it a robust adaptive immune response,
and there's a man somewhere who was proud of that sentence.
So I moved through a bay full of sick people at full strength, looking down at them from a height
nobody asked me to have, and there is no version of that where you come out clean. I couldn't be
sick with them. It's the only thing they all had in common that week and I couldn't have it. Every
morning I was fine and fifty people weren't, and the fine was doing more damage to my standing
in that room than the ears ever did.
* * *
Then came the weapons.
The armory was a temple of oil and cold metal. We filed past and the armorer, a grizzled specialist
with sergeant's stripes, called assignments. "Smith, M4. Gonzalez, M4. Carter, M4."
He reached me. His eyes flicked from the manifest up to my face - a longer journey than for most -
then to a different, heavier rack.
"Renaya. Mk 13 Mod 7. .300 Winchester Magnum. Bolt-action precision rifle. With this."
He handed it over.
It was a beast. Over four feet long, thick fluted barrel, massive muzzle brake, chunky telescopic
sight. The weight settled into my hands - a dense profound thirteen pounds of steel and polymer
and glass. Less a weapon than a piece of industrial machinery meant to be mounted on something.
Compared to the M4s the others cradled like toys, it was from another world.
In my hands, its scale made sense. It didn't look oversized. It looked correct.
I stood there in a cold room holding the first object I have ever been handed that was the right
size, and I want to say I felt nothing about that, and I did feel something about it, and I have never
liked what.
A stunned silence, then a sharp collective intake of breath. Heads turned. A recruit named Finch,
two people ahead, craned his neck, eyes wide as saucers.
Before he could blink, the armorer's voice cracked like a whip. "FINCH! YOU SEE SOMETHING
PRETTY? YOU ON A TOUR? YOUR WEAPON IS IN YOUR HANDS! EYES ON YOUR OWN
GEAR UNLESS YOU WANT TO CLEAN EVERY WEAPON IN THIS ARMORY WITH A
TOOTHBRUSH!"
Finch's head snapped forward so fast I heard his neck pop.
The lesson was immediate: staring at the anomaly is a failure of focus.
It became a pattern. Zeroing on the range, settling in behind the rifle, I'd feel eyes. A flicker in my
peripheral vision.
"HILL! YOU TRYING TO LEARN BY OSMOSIS? WISHING WON'T MAKE YOUR GROUPING
TIGHTER! FOCUS ON YOUR FRONT SIGHT POST OR I'LL HAVE YOU WEARING IT AS AN
EARRING!"
On a march, someone stumbling behind me would gawk at the rifle on my shoulder or the tip of
my tail keeping time with the walk.
"VALDEZ! THE ROAD IS IN FRONT OF YOU, NOT ON HER BACK! YOU LOST? NEED A MAP?
PICK UP THE PACE AND WATCH YOUR SECTOR!"
The DIs never mocked me for being looked at. They mocked the others for looking.
It took me a while to see it whole, and when I did I had to admire it, the way you admire a trap
that's already closed on you. They never had to say don't call her a dog. They never issued a rule
about it. They simply made looking at me expensive, and the men stopped looking, and the
discipline they were building got built out of the material I was standing there being.
My difference wasn't a source of personal humiliation anymore. It was a test for everyone else. A
distraction to be overcome. A temptation to fail. Every head that turned my way was an excuse for
a DI to pounce, punish, reinforce the only thing that mattered: the task.
My presence became a metric of their discipline. I was equipment they were being graded on
ignoring.
I couldn't decide then whether that was kindness. I've had years and I still can't. It worked. It got
me quiet. It cost them nothing and it cost me the last thing I had, which was the possibility that
one of those fifty people might have looked at me on purpose, for a reason of his own.
* * *
The drawback of the Mk 13 was constant and grinding. So was the advantage, and it showed up
during a tactical drill.
We were simulating perimeter guard. The DI pointed at me. "Renaya! That cannon is a liability
inside ten meters. Simulate a security hand-off. Briggs! Secure her primary!"
Briggs had muscles on his muscles and stood a good eight inches shorter. He stepped forward. His
eyes darted from my face to the rifle - that same flicker of awe and apprehension.
"BRIGGS! YOU TAKING A CENSUS OF HER FEATURES OR SECURING A WEAPON? MOVE!"
He snapped to, but the damage was done. He was already off-balance.
I unslung the Mk 13 and passed it over. The moment the full weight transferred his body jerked,
eyes widening as his posture sank, arms tightening instinctively to wrestle the unexpected heft.
"Looks like a struggle, Briggs! You admiring the craftsmanship or fighting a bear? Renaya, you're
weaponless! Demonstrate a hostile take-down. Non-lethal. Go."
Unburdened by thirteen pounds of anchor, my body became pure potential.
I was across the gap in one long stride before he could reset. I used his rooted unbalanced posture
against him, my longer arms bypassing his guard, swept his legs, drove him to the mat with a
controlled thud.
Controlled. I want that word to stand, because I put it there deliberately and it is doing a lot of
work.
I did not decide any of that. Not one piece of it. The word go left the man's mouth and something
in me that is faster than I am read Briggs's stance, found the weight on his heels, and took him
down, and by the time I arrived the job was finished and my hands were empty and a man was on
his back looking up at me.
The controlled was the only part I contributed. Somewhere in the middle of it, whatever runs that
machinery let me put a hand on the throttle, and I have thought about that a great deal since. It let
me. It could have not.
I stood there on a mat in a forest with fifty people watching and understood, in a way the clinical
never managed, what they actually put in me.
The DI didn't praise. He narrated, voice cutting through the damp air while the platoon stood rigid
with their eyes fixed on nothing.
"Observe. The hammer is heavy. The arm is built to carry it. A tool for every task." He let the
silence hang. "Briggs, firewatch. The rest of you, look alive - at your own damn jobs."
The arm is built to carry it.
It was a visceral, undeniable lesson. My different was a tactical specification, and noticing it was a
punishable lapse.
The recruits learned. The stares didn't stop; they went covert, fleeting, followed by an immediate
paranoid snap back to whatever they were supposed to be doing.
I wasn't a spectacle anymore. I was a hazard. A distraction hazard.
It was the most anonymity I'd had since I got there. I'll take it. That's the sentence I keep arriving
at and I've stopped fighting it: they turned me into a piece of equipment that fifty men were
punished for perceiving, and I'll take it, because the alternative was Fido.
* * *
The only respite was food.
The chow hall was a haven of shocking greasy abundance. The food was, against all odds,
awesome. Giant portions of pasta, real eggs, mountains of potatoes. Calories as fuel, and we were
furnaces. My metabolism and my size demanded a shocking amount of it. I ate with mechanical
focus, often going back for seconds while others picked at their plates. Even the MREs were
acceptable in their weird vacuum-sealed way.
At the table, for fifteen minutes at a time, we weren't maggots. We were hungry animals refueling
in silence.
We. Fifteen minutes a day, three times, and it's the only place in this account where I get to use
that word about a room with other people in it.
I sat alone, as always, at the end of a table. But the space around me had stopped feeling like exile
and started feeling like a cleared range. Nobody looked too long. Nobody sat too close. I could eat
in peace, the monstrous rifle leaning against the table like a silent guardian.
I was the one who carried the thirteen-pound answer, and I was built on a scale that made it look
logical. And if you were smart, you kept your eyes on your own tray.
I took a bite of gravy-soaked meatloaf. It was fuel. For now that was enough.
The rest was just physics, and I was finally starting to feel like I was built for the equation - which
is a sentence I would have found horrifying two months earlier, and which I thought that evening
with something I'd have to call contentment.
The silence of their averted eyes was the closest thing to respect I was likely to get. I did the
arithmetic on that too, at that table, with my mouth full.
It came out yes.

## Chapter 10 - The Trials
The gas chamber was a small concrete-block building that smelled, even from outside, like dread
and old chemicals.
We lined up in our protective masks, the tight rubber seals alien against my face, pressing my ears
flat. Sergeant Hale stood by the door, his own mask making him look like a giant unfeeling insect.
"This is CS gas. Tear gas. It will not kill you. It will make you wish you were dead. The point is to
trust your gear, and to learn how to clear and seal your mask under stress. You will enter, form a
line, and on my command, you will break the seal, remove your mask, state your name and rank,
then reseal. Anyone who runs for the door before I say 'go' repeats. Anyone who doesn't complete
the exercise repeats. Understood?"
"YES, SERGEANT!"
One by one we filed into the thick hazy air. Even masked, my eyes started to water. The air had a
sharp metallic taste that seeped through.
Then my turn.
"RENAYA! BREAK SEAL AND REMOVE!"
I pulled the mask off my face.
The world dissolved into instant agonizing fire. Like inhaling crushed glass and hornets. My lungs
seized, a raw scraping cough tearing out of me. My eyes flooded, vision smearing into grey walls
and green uniforms.
And it was exactly as bad as it was for everyone else in that room.
No worse. No special weakness the lab had built in. Just the same chemical hell, democratically
applied. It sat in my sinuses and the back of my throat a little longer - sharper senses, slower to
stop screaming - but that's a rounding error.
The great equalizer. In there I was one more body drowning in its own mucus.
"NAME AND RANK!" Hale bellowed from somewhere in the burning fog.
I tried to suck air to speak and got a mouthful of fire. "RENAYA-!" A strangled rasp before a
coughing fit doubled me over. Snot pouring from my nose, thick and uncontrollable.
All around me the sounds were the same. Retching, gagging, the wet desperate hacking of fifty
people drowning in their own mucus. The respiratory plague that had haunted the bay for a week
was instantly, violently cured. Nobody was congested anymore. Every passage ruthlessly, painfully
clear.
I'd spent a week being the only healthy body in a sick room. It took a concrete shed full of poison
gas to get me admitted.
"SEAL YOUR MASK!"
I fumbled the rubber back over my face, fingers slick with tears and snot, and sucked in the sweet
filtered air. Instantaneous relief, though the burning lingered another hour, the way it did for
everyone.
We stumbled out into the clean cold daylight, faces raw and shining, all of us united in a new
profound understanding of misery.
For a few minutes, nobody looked at me. They were too busy trying to remember how to breathe.
* * *
Night ops came a week later.
They issued night-vision goggles - heavy bulky units that turned the world into a ghostly green
landscape of vague shapes and halos. We practiced moving in squads, communicating in whispers,
the gear hissing and whining in our ears.
I tried to use them. I did try.
The amplified light was overwhelming, washing out detail, and the limited field of view was like
looking through two foggy soda bottles. It was harder to see with them than without.
During a navigation exercise, moving through dense woods, I finally lifted them onto my forehead.
The world didn't just brighten. It clarified.
The darkness wasn't an obstacle. It was a different palette. Deep shadows held texture and depth. I
could tell the grey of a rock from the grey of a root, see the path of crushed leaves, track the heat-
shimmer of a recruit ten yards off as a faint rippling disturbance in the cool air. My pupils
gathered every stray photon. I could hear the lieutenant's whispered commands from two squads
over with perfect clarity.
I read the real night like a cloudy twilight, and fifty people around me stumbled through an
electronic haze.
"Renaya, why are your NODs stowed?" A hiss. Corporal Vance, training cadre.
"They degrade my natural sight, Corporal. My night vision is sufficient."
A long pause. I could feel his stare in the dark, and he couldn't see my face at all, and there was
something in that worth having.
"Noted. Keep up. And don't make me regret this."
I didn't make him regret it.
By the end of the exercise I was quietly directing my squad around obstacles I could see and they
couldn't, my whispers keeping them out of ditches and off low branches. Nobody thanked me. But
when we reassembled, ours was the only squad without a sprained ankle or a face full of
spiderwebs.
The looks weren't fear or disgust anymore. They were grim practical acknowledgment.
I'll take that trade too. I was starting to notice how many trades I'd taken.
* * *
The PT tests were where it shifted, almost imperceptibly.
Straightforward: two-mile run, max push-ups in two minutes, max sit-ups in two, pull-ups. For
me they were laughable. My body performed with a cold efficiency that unsettled even me.
But standing there panting lightly after the run, watching others stagger across the line with faces
purple from strain, something strange happened.
I didn't feel smug. I felt impatient.
"Come on, Miller! You've got fifty yards! Dig! It's just pain, leave it on the road!"
The words were out of my mouth before I thought about them. A full sentence, at volume, in a
yard, at a man who'd called me Fido.
Miller shot me a look of pure shock. Then he grimaced and found a last desperate sprint.
On the pull-up bar I watched Carter fail his seventh rep, arms shaking. "Don't let your knees drift,
Carter! Lock your core! You've got three more in you, I know you do!"
He grunted, heaved, got an eighth.
It wasn't kindness. I checked. I checked immediately, standing right there, because if it was
kindness then something in me had come unlatched without permission and I needed to know.
It wasn't. It was pragmatism, and brutal. We were cells in an organism. If the cell next to me is
weak, the whole body is weaker. My own performance is irrelevant if the platoon fails. The DIs had
beaten that into us for weeks.
For the first time I internalized it - not as a threat. As a mechanic of survival.
That's what I told myself in the yard and I was satisfied with it, and I would like to now point out
the flaw, since nobody else was going to.
I wasn't part of the organism. I never had been. That was the whole design - red tape on a seat, ten
feet off the formation, you are not of this platoon. If the platoon failed, the attachment did not fail
with it. I had checked that months ago, the way I check everything.
So the pragmatic argument for shouting at Miller was worth exactly nothing. It was a false proof I
built at speed to cover the fact that I wanted him to make it.
I didn't see that for years.
* * *
Slowly, tentatively, I became able to lead. Not by appointment. By default.
On ruck marches, when somebody's pack strap was digging in, I'd bark the adjustment drill Hale
taught us. During the endless mind-numbing parade drills, when the rank lost its dressing, my
height and voice from the flank called the alignment back. "Dress right, DRESS! Hold your
interval, Garcia!"
And the most profound comfort in that entire forest was the silence from the DIs.
They never blamed me for being different. Not once, in a month. They only ever blamed the others
for noticing.
"YOU STARING AT HER FEET TO LEARN THE CADENCE, JENKINS? EYES FRONT!"
That simple consistent reinforcement was a balm I hadn't known I needed. My existence was not
the problem. Their lack of focus was.
Twelve years. That's how long I'd waited for someone in authority to look at a room and locate the
problem somewhere other than my body. It took a defense contractor with a training schedule to
do it, and they did it for the wrong reasons, and it worked anyway. I don't know what to do with
that. I still don't.
We did ruck marches that felt like they'd never end, the Mk 13 an old familiar enemy on my
shoulder. Hand-to-hand in the gritty pits, where my reach and speed became assets I learned to
control instead of flail. Parade drills until the movements were autonomic, tail held in a tight still
curl against my leg. A form of camping they called bivouacking, which was mostly lying in a cold
damp hole trying not to shiver, listening to a forest with ears that catch every rustle and snap.
I was not becoming one of them. That was never the goal. The red-taped seat still existed.
But the space around me had stopped being a vacuum of hostility. It had become a space of
function. I was a unique tool in the kit and the other tools were slowly, grudgingly, learning to
work alongside me.
And I was starting to care whether they could do their jobs, which surprised me more than
anything that happened in that forest.
The withering internal narration - freak, monster, mistake - was getting drowned out by the
immediate demands of the task. Move faster. Shoot straighter. Carry more. Help them.
The thing in my blood, the one that drove me across a rooftop to a stranger, was being channeled.
Given a direction. Given a pack, however dysfunctional.
I noticed the shape of that at the time and let it go by, and I'd like to pick it up now.
On the roof, that thing wanted a person, and I called it a malfunction and swallowed something to
make it stop. Here it wanted fifty people, and they gave it a rifle and a rank and told me it was
leadership.
It isn't that they cured me. Nobody cured anything, and nobody medicated me either - I'd taken
exactly one tablet in my life and that was two months and two hundred miles ago. They found the
appetite the lab installed and pointed it at a mission, and that was all it took. And it worked.
That's the part I can't get around even now. I was less alone in that hole in the ground than I'd
been since I was six, and every bit of it was issued to me.
A different kind of cage. But for the first time in my life the walls weren't only there to contain me.
They were there to shape me into something that could, perhaps, eventually break out.
I believed that. I want it in the record that I believed it, standing in a forest at twenty, holding a
rifle that fit.

# Part 3 ~ Blood Relation
## Chapter 1 - The Hunting Lodge
The lodge smelled like cedar and old smoke. The agency picked it for me on purpose - a hunting
lodge at the dead end of a logging road, the kind of place where a tall woman who comes and goes
at strange hours and brings back strange cargo is just a hunter. Nothing more. A hunter living in a
hunting lodge. Nobody looks twice. That is the entire point of it, and I have never decided whether
I resent how well it works.
The walls are cedar gone honey-dark with age. Taxidermy everywhere - a whitetail buck over the
cold fireplace, a brace of pheasants frozen mid-flush, a bobcat on a shelf with glass eyes that catch
the light better than the real ones ever did. An antler chandelier hangs over the main room, a
dozen sheds wired together into something meant to read as rustic luxury, which mostly reads as a
lot of dead animals over your head while you eat. I didn't choose a single piece of it. The agency
dressed the set before I moved in. I've only added to it, here and there, when a job left me
something worth keeping.
My equipment fits the lie without trying. The charge rifle stands in the rack by the door, between a
hunting bow and a lever-action that has never been fired. The lead-lined containment boxes
stacked in the mudroom look like gun cases. The breaching tool hangs on a pegboard of axes and
saws and looks like family. A stranger walking through would see a hunter's home and nothing
underneath it.
The disguise is the house. The house is good. I live inside a costume that is also a building.
I got back near dawn. The job was done and bagged - routine, contained, nothing I'd lose sleep
over. The thing I pulled out of it sat by the door in a vault box, sealed, quiet now that the lid was
shut. I'd run it to headquarters in the morning and not think about it again.
I put a can in the fridge and took the cold one already in there. Four left on the shelf. I have never
once been surprised by that number.
So I told myself.
It wasn't the box I kept circling back to.
It was the photograph on my nightstand.
I found it months ago, on another job - a house at the edge of a town that had thinned out around
it, empty for decades, behind cracked glass in a hallway nobody had walked in twenty years. I took
it off the wall without deciding to. That should have told me something. I do not do things without
deciding to; the whole architecture of me is built to prevent exactly that. I took it anyway, and I
haven't been able to put it down since. It travels from coat pocket to table to the nightstand by my
bed, where I set it most nights before I sleep, the way some people set down a watch. I've told
myself a dozen times to log it and let it go.
I picked it up and stood over it in my coat, contacts already in, looking at it the way I look at
everything I don't understand yet - which is to say, too long, and from too many angles, as though
the answer is a matter of finding the correct approach.
The colors have gone bad with the years. Reds drained, blues turned green, the whole thing sitting
behind its cracked glass like it gave up trying to be a photograph. The face didn't go with the
colors. A young woman, hair parted in the middle, smiling at someone off-frame. The eyes aren't
red. The ears are ordinary.
But the bones underneath are a face I have been looking at in my own mirror my whole life
without knowing whose it was.
I don't have a name for the feeling. I have names for very few of them; it's a gap in the instrument
I've never bothered to close, because for most of my life nothing has come up that required the
distinction. This did. And somewhere in the night I'd decided: today I take it to Vanity, and stop
pretending I don't need to know.
Headlights swept the front windows. Gravel under tires. Korval, on time, the way he is always on
time.
I slid the photograph into the inside pocket of my coat, against my chest, and picked up the box.
Outside, the cold met my face and my ears flattened back under the hat by themselves. I let them.
Korval's SUV idled in the turnaround, exhaust ghosting white in the dark.
"Morning," he said as I set the box on the back seat.
"Headquarters." I got in. "I have something to report. And something that isn't a report."
He didn't ask. He never asks until I want him to, and I have never worked out whether that's
discipline or disinterest, and I have never asked either, which probably answers it.
He pulled out onto the logging road. The lodge shrank in the side mirror - cedar and dark glass,
the antler chandelier a faint warm smudge through the front window where I'd left one lamp
burning.
Then the trees took it, and there was only the road, and the box, and the photograph against my
chest like a second heartbeat I hadn't asked for.

## Chapter 2 - The Address
The containment lounge sits between the vault corridor and the rest of the building. Long tables.
Padded chairs. Coffee that's always on. Low-light displays along one wall showing the current
inventory of the vaults, color-coded by threat tier. A ficus in the corner that has been alive longer
than I've been with the agency, which I find obscurely reassuring and have never examined too
closely.
I came in through the rear door with the box.
The desk officer took it without comment. Weighed it. Scanned it. Logged it. The box went
through the slot in the wall and was gone. I would not think about it again.
So I told myself.
Vanity was in the lounge.
She stood near the long table, hands clasped behind her back, looking at one of the wall displays.
She didn't turn when I came in. She didn't need to. The corner of her mouth had moved before I
was fully through the door - she'd had me on some sensor since the parking level, and the mouth
was a courtesy, staged for my benefit at the correct moment. She does that. I've never decided
whether it's kindness or theater. Probably it doesn't have to be one.
"Operator."
"Vanity."
She's the only one in the building who can make a rank sound like a greeting instead of an order.
She's also the only one I answer with a first name instead of a surname. Neither arrangement was
ever negotiated out loud, which is how I know it's real. The things I've agreed to in words have
mostly been things done to me.
"Sit. You smell like cedar and animal furs."
"I smell like home."
"You smell like a hunting lodge that hasn't been aired out in twenty years."
I sat. She moved to the chair opposite, deliberately a half-beat slow - the android courtesy of
pretending to be a person who needs a moment. A small folder sat on the table between us. She
slid it across.
"That's the woman in your photograph."
I hadn't given her a name to work with. I didn't have one. I'd handed her a cracked frame off a wall
in a dead house and she'd given me back a person, and I knew how - she'd have run the face, the
same as she'd run a plate - but knowing the method doesn't do much for you when the output is a
stranger who has your bones.
I opened it.
A color photograph paper-clipped to the corner: an older woman in a sun hat, in a garden, smiling
at someone off-camera.
It was the face from the photograph in my coat, forty years later.
It was my own face, forty years older.
I handled it well, which I mention because nobody was there to see. I sat there and did arithmetic
on a stranger's cheekbones, and the arithmetic kept coming out the same, and I made no sound at
all.
"Dr. Rosalyn Renaya," Vanity said.
She let the surname sit there. Didn't lean on it. Didn't pause for me to catch up, which she could
have, and which I'd have hated.
Renaya is mine off my father, and my father is a man I have never met and never wondered about
out loud, because a name on foster paperwork isn't a person, it's a field. I've carried his name the
way you carry a coat somebody left in your car. It had never occurred to me it came from
anywhere - that there was a woman it came through, who was alive, and had a garden.
"Sixty-eight. Lives alone, two hours from here. Kindergarten teacher 1985 through 1995, a few
towns from where you were born. Went back to medical school in her late thirties. Pediatrician
now. Doesn't lead with the title. Widowed. No other children of record. Her son and daughter-in-
law - your biological parents, per the foster paperwork on you - left the area when you were small.
Their records thin out after that and don't come back. Her record on you ends at the foster
system."
"The house you took it out of," I said. "The one on the job."
"Four miles from the school she taught at." She said it the way she says everything, which is
without any weight on it at all. "The town had people in it once. Whoever lived in that hallway
knew her well enough to hang her on a wall. A cousin. A colleague. A friend from church. I can
find out. It'll take a week and it will not be interesting."
Somebody in that hallway had known a young schoolteacher well enough to put her behind glass.
Then they'd died, or left, and the glass went on hanging in the dark for twenty years.
"It's not a coincidence," Vanity said, reading me the way she does. "It's a small county. You were
born in it. Everything in that county is four miles from everything else. The coincidence is that you
were the one who took it off the wall, and I can't run a file on that."
I read the address. It's a normal address. That's what I couldn't get past - that it had been a normal
address this whole time, printable, two hours away, sitting in a file that took Vanity an afternoon.
"She's alive."
"She's alive."
"And she's been alone."
"For a while."
I closed the folder. Put both hands flat on it.
"I want to go see her."
"I assumed."
"How."
"You take it as leave. Real leave, not a job with a family on the end of it. Korval drives you. I clear
the paperwork. The agency calls her ahead of time so a stranger doesn't pull up to her door and
shorten her life by ten years."
"Have you called her."
"Not yet. I wanted to ask you first."
I looked at her. She could have called. She had the number before she had the folder, and she'd sat
on it, because there is a difference between having a thing done to you and choosing it, and she is
the only person alive who has never needed that explained.
"Call her."
"Today?"
"Today."
She inclined her head - a small mechanical motion that meant acknowledged, and also meant I'm
proud of you, and also meant don't make me say it. She could produce a warmer gesture. She has
the hardware. She uses the small one because she knows I can read it and knows I'd leave the
room if she used the other.
"Take the week. More if you need it. The queue will hold."
"And you'll log it as what."
"Personal. You're not on a clock and you're not reporting in. Call me when you feel like it - once a
day, so I know you're alive. That's all I want." A half-beat. "Just your voice down the line. That'll
do."
"I can do that."
I stood. The folder went into my bag with the photograph from the house. Two pieces of Rosalyn -
one from 1985, one from an agency record - and soon a third one that breathed.
"Operator."
"Yeah."
"Wear the dress. Wear the hat. Wear the contacts. Until you don't have to."
"I know."
"And then take them off."
"I know."

## Chapter 3 - The Visit
I was nervous, which took me most of an hour to identify.
That sounds absurd. I have been shot at. I know what fear does in my body down to the second -
the drop, the narrowing, the way sound goes flat and useful. This wasn't that. This was diffuse and
pointless and had no object I could name, and I sat in it a long time before I got a word onto it,
because there was nothing to fight and nothing to leave and so the instrument had nothing to
point at.
I looked out the window while Korval drove. He follows me everywhere. Not that I need it. I am
usually the dangerous one in any room, and both of us know it, and neither of us has ever said so.
The agency had turned up word of a biological family member and given me the opportunity to
visit. I'd taken it. My grandmother. Rosalyn Renaya.
"How much further until we get there?" The words came out before I'd decided to say them, which
was the second bad sign in an hour.
"About thirty minutes," Korval said, the accent thick as ever. Then: "Why are you so impatient?
You're always calm."
I had no reply. I'd never wanted to be close to family before - there'd been nobody to want it at, so
the wanting had never had anywhere to go. Now there was somewhere, and it turned out the
wanting had been sitting there the whole time, fully assembled, waiting for an address.
I stared out the window as the metropolis turned into rural neighborhoods.
"Okay, we're just about there," Korval said. "Make sure you dress appropriately."
I had nearly forgotten that my grandmother didn't know about my altered form.
I climbed into the back seat and changed my jeans for a dress. Put a hat on. Put the contacts in.
I felt like a completely different person. I could not tell which version was the real one. I have
never been able to, and I have stopped expecting the question to resolve itself on a drive.
* * *
It was a warm day. The sun was out. In the window glass I looked like somebody's daughter on her
way to lunch, and that was the point of the exercise, and I did not enjoy how well it worked.
* * *
The house was covered in ivy and looked a little overgrown, in the way of a place tended by
someone who has decided which battles are worth it. A small stone path led to the front door.
When we parked, an old lady walked out.
"I'm Osyra, and you're Rosalyn, right? My grandmother?"
"Ah yes, I got a phone call saying that you would be around soon."
"That must have been my boss."
"Lieutenant something. I can't remember his name."
"He worries too much."
"Well, that means he cares about you, honey. We can talk about it inside. Does your driver want to
come in too?"
I gave Korval a subconscious side eye. "No. He prefers the quiet."
Korval said nothing. He just nodded slightly, then walked to the front of the car and sat on the
hood. His sunglasses reflecting.
"He'll head to a hotel when it gets dark. Back in the morning," I said.
"All right then, sweetheart." Rosalyn turned around and walked inside.
I looked back at Korval for a second, nodded, and followed my grandmother in.
Once inside I took a seat close to the door.
I told myself it was manners - that I didn't want to wander into a stranger's home uninvited. That
held up for about four seconds. It was the door. It's always the door. I do not sit down in a room
without knowing the count of exits, and I was doing it here, in an ivy-covered house belonging to a
sixty-eight-year-old pediatrician, against the possibility that I would need to leave her living room
at speed. I considered getting up and moving to the couch, just to prove I could.
I didn't. I stayed where I was and let myself have the door, and told myself I'd move later, which is
what I always tell myself.
The seat was almost too comfortable. Age had let it relax into the exact shape of a body. The light
wasn't blinding and wasn't dull. The wood trim, the furniture - all of it a snapshot back in time.
The place smelled like newspaper and sunshine.
It felt like a memory, which was not possible. I have never been in that house. But something in
the back of my head kept insisting it recognized the room, and I couldn't make it stop, and after a
while I stopped trying and just sat in the chair that knew what a person was shaped like.
The plan was simple: stay the night, see what the morning looked like. There was no job at the end
of this one. Korval would be back after breakfast, and after that the days were mine - the first time
in years that sentence had been true.
"What do you do for work, dear?"
"Can't say much. It's military."
"Oh, I understand. I don't want to get you in any trouble," Rosalyn said softly.
"Combat unit. Mostly eliminating dangerous animals."
"What a world we live in. They come up with new jobs every day, don't they?" She smiled. "As long
as they're paying you well and treating you right."
"They are," I said.
"How about some pictures? So I can remember you once this day's over." She'd been wandering
the whole time we talked. She reached onto a counter and came up with a Polaroid.
My face went red.
I don't take pictures. There's an operational reason and I reached for it first, because it's clean and
it's true and it isn't the reason. Then I got to the actual problem, which was this: she wanted a
picture of her granddaughter, and the thing sitting in her chair was a woman in a hat with brown
contacts over red eyes. If I said yes she'd get a photograph of somebody who doesn't exist. She'd
put it on the shelf with her mother in the high collar. Forty years from now somebody would find
it and never know they were looking at a costume.
I could take the hat off. The thought arrived fully formed and I looked at it the way you look at a
live wire on wet ground. Right there, in the good chair, in the light, two hours from the lodge and
thirty seconds from finding out whether the whole day had been a mistake. She'd have flinched or
she wouldn't. That's all it would have cost. One second of information.
I have made harder decisions in worse rooms with people shooting at me.
"Later," I said.
"Of course, honey. You must be tired."
She drifted toward the kitchen, murmuring about the kettle. I stayed in the chair and let her go,
and sat with the fact that I'd just declined to find out something I'd driven two hours to learn. I
told myself it was pacing. Give her the afternoon first. Ease into it. That's a strategy, and it is also
what a coward says, and both were true at once and I couldn't get a blade between them.
I'd call Vanity tonight, the way I'd been told. Once a day, so somebody at headquarters knew I was
still breathing. Just my voice down the line, and Vanity on the other end taking down whatever I
felt like saying, patient, keeping it for the record.
She brought in tea eventually. I had trouble paying attention. The chair was pulling me under, my
body slowing the way it never slows, and I kept waiting for the tea to fix it and it didn't. I'd been
running so long I hadn't noticed I was tired. I mean that plainly. I had no idea. The instrument
doesn't point inward, and it had been reading fine for years while the answer was no.
I took the cup almost greedily. Anything to stay awake for this.
Chamomile.
I got about half of it down before the joke landed on me. She hadn't brought me anything to stay
awake on. There was nothing in that cup but hot water and a flower, and I'd been drinking it like a
stimulant and waiting to be fixed by it.
Which was fine. There were two cans in the truck and I knew exactly where, and I'd have got up
for one at any point in the last hour and hadn't, because getting up meant leaving the room. I sat
there and went without and said nothing about it.
She hadn't asked. I want to be careful here, because I have watched people be kind at me for
twenty-nine years and I know what it looks like when someone has done their homework and
wants credit for it. That wasn't this. There was no glance to see if I'd noticed. She'd looked at a
woman who'd driven two hours and hadn't slept and was sitting in her front room with her hands
wrong, and she'd gone and boiled the thing you give somebody who needs to come down.
Forty years of small frightened people, and it comes out of her hands automatically, and she
doesn't know she's doing it.
She watched me drink for a moment. Then she set her own cup down on the side table and went to
the bookshelf at the far end of the room. Her fingers ran along the spines until they stopped on
something thick and softbound. She pulled it out with both hands.
"I dug this out before you came," she said. "Thought you might want to see."
She brought it back over and lowered herself into the chair across from me. The album opened
with a soft creak of old leather. Pages thick as cardboard. The kind with little black corners stuck
on to hold each photo in place. She turned to the first page and angled it toward me.
"This is your great-grandmother. My mother. She was a tough old thing."
I leaned forward enough to see. A black-and-white photograph of a woman in a high-collared
dress, hair pulled back so tight it looked painful. She wasn't smiling. None of them did, in those
days.
"She looks strict," I said.
"Oh, she was. But she'd give you the last bite off her plate if she thought you needed it more." She
smiled and turned the page. A few more faces. Names I didn't catch. People who had lived and
died long before I ever drew a breath.
I let her go. It was easier than answering. The chair was pulling at me and her voice had a rhythm,
like wind through a screen door.
She turned another page, and the rhythm changed.
"And this," she said, more carefully now, "is your mother."
I looked.
A young woman, sitting on the steps of a porch. Thin. Tired in a way that didn't belong on a face
that young. She was holding something in her lap - a bundle. A baby. She wasn't looking at the
camera. She was looking down at the bundle.
I knew the porch.
That's what got me. Not the woman. The porch - the paint coming off the third step in a shape like
a bird. I have put my hand on that step. I couldn't tell you when or why, but the knowing came up
out of my body without asking permission, the way a smell does, and it was certain, and it was
worth nothing.
I looked at the face and got nothing back. Six years of a person and all I kept was a step.
I've been told this is normal, at various points, by various people paid to tell me things. That
nobody keeps much from before seven. That the mind is still deciding what it is. It has not once
helped to be told, and it doesn't now, and I have never understood why people offer the
explanation as though it were the same thing as the memory.
"How old was she?" I asked.
"Just barely an adult." Rosalyn touched the edge of the photograph with one finger. "She and your
father - they didn't have anything, sweetheart. Not really. Not enough for a child. They tried, for a
while. But there's a kind of poor that doesn't bend, and they were in it."
I nodded. There wasn't anything to say to that.
I took another sip of the tea.
She didn't turn the page. She sat with her hand flat on it, the way you hold a door you haven't
decided to open.
"They wouldn't let me near you," she said. "That's the bit I've never gotten all the way around. I
knew you were born. I knew where. She'd answer the phone maybe one time in five, and she was
always about to call me back." Her thumb moved once against the paper. "I think she couldn't
stand for me to see it. The state of things. She'd have rather starved than had her mother-in-law
standing in that kitchen counting what wasn't in it. And I let her have that. Six years, I let her have
her pride, because I kept telling myself I'd get one more chance to ask."
She said it evenly, which was worse than if she hadn't.
"Then a school nurse filled out a form," Rosalyn said. "Some woman who'd never met any of us,
doing her job, writing down what she saw. And that was enough to take you. But not one person in
that whole process thought to ask whether the child had anybody." She looked at her hands. "They
had the name. It's the same name. It was in the phone book."
I looked at the woman on the porch steps instead of at her. It seemed easier for both of us.
"You couldn't have known they'd take me," I said.
"No," Rosalyn agreed. "But I could have driven down. Any Tuesday of those six years, I could have
driven down and knocked, and I didn't, because she'd have been embarrassed." She let out a
breath and it shook coming out, and she got it back under control the way a person does when
they've had years of practice. "That's the whole of it, sweetheart. I was being polite. I'm sorry. You
didn't come here to hold this for me."
Then, quieter, not really to me at all: "There's no picture of you after that one. I've looked. Not one
anywhere in this house, because nobody was taking any."
The album stayed open on the porch steps a moment longer than it needed to. Then she turned
the page.
The next photo stopped me for a different reason.
A man - middle-aged, broad-shouldered - standing in a clearing somewhere wooded. A rifle
propped against his shoulder. A deer at his feet, freshly taken. He was smiling, but it was a tight
kind of smile, like he wasn't comfortable with the camera.
"My husband," she said. "He's been gone a long time now. He'd have liked you, I think. He used to
do something a bit like what you do - tracked nuisance animals, mostly. Bears that got into the
trash. The occasional cat that started taking livestock."
Something went tight in my chest. Of course he did.
"He'd ask you a thousand questions about it," she went on. "What kind of rifle, what range you
work at, how often you have to take a shot at running game. He always wanted to know more than
he let on."
"It's mostly close work," I said. Carefully. "Tracking. Containment. The shooting part isn't the hard
part."
"That's what he used to say." She laughed once. "Said anyone could pull a trigger. Hard part was
being sure you should."
I made myself smile. Not enough to show teeth. "Yeah."
She watched me for a half-second longer than felt natural. Then she turned the page.
A few more photos. A house she used to live in. A garden that was no longer there. Then she closed
the album in her lap and rested both hands flat on the cover.
"I've been hoping someone would call for years," she said. She said it like she was admitting to a
small thing, not a heavy one. "I knew about you, you know. Always knew there was a
granddaughter out there somewhere. Just couldn't ever find the thread to pull."
I didn't know what to do with that. I sat with the cup in my lap and stared at it.
"This place gets quiet," she said. "Quieter than I thought it would. The phone doesn't ring much.
My hands aren't what they were. Some mornings I open jars with the back of a butter knife." She
laughed, soft. "Don't tell anyone."
"I won't."
She looked at me a moment longer, then patted the album once, gently, like saying goodnight to it.
"You look tired, honey."
I was tired.
"Why don't you take the back room. The one at the end of the hall. The bed's made up. I'd been
hoping you might."
I nodded. I didn't trust my voice.
I set the cup down, stood up, and let her show me the way.

## Chapter 4 - The Reveal
The back room was small. Wood paneling, a single bed made up with a hand-stitched quilt, a low
dresser, a window looking out into the dark of the yard. A second door on the right opened onto a
bathroom - I could see a strip of tile through the gap.
"Towels are under the sink," she said. "Hot water takes a minute. Give it a minute."
"Thank you."
"There's a nightshirt in the top drawer if it fits. Was mine, a long time ago. You're a fair bit taller
than I ever was, but it might do." She paused at the doorway. "I'll be in the kitchen. Tea if you
want it. Otherwise - sleep well, sweetheart."
She closed the door behind her.
I stood in the middle of the room and listened. Her footsteps moved off down the hall. A cabinet
opened. A faucet ran. Then the slow shuffling sound of a woman who's walked the same
floorboards long enough to do it without looking.
I let out a breath I hadn't known I was holding.
* * *
I undressed in pieces.
The hat first. I set it on the dresser the way you set down something fragile. My ears came up and
gave a slow twitch - they'd been flat for hours and the muscle at the base of them was aching. I
rubbed at it with both hands. A small, embarrassing relief.
Then the dress. The hem caught on my tail where it always catches. I shook it loose and folded it
on the chair, and my tail uncurled from against my leg where I'd kept it tucked all day, and the
circulation came back in that familiar prickle.
I sat on the edge of the bed and worked the contacts out, one at a time, blinking them into the
case. The world went crisper. Reds went redder.
I looked in the dresser mirror. Really looked, for the first time all day.
Long ears. Long tail. Red eyes catching the lamplight in a way no human eye does.
I looked like myself.
There's a version of this where I say I hated how much I'd missed it, and that would be tidy, and it
isn't accurate. What I hated was the arithmetic. Twelve hours in the costume and I come out of it
feeling like a person - which means the thirteen years I've spent calling the costume practical were
thirteen years of paying a bill I told myself I wasn't paying. I'd rather have not known the number.
* * *
The shower helped.
I stood under it longer than I needed to. Hot water on the back of my neck, down the muscles
along my spine, on the place between my shoulder blades that always carries something I can't
name. The fur on my ears and tail flattened out and went darker. I washed my hair twice. No
reason. It felt good.
When I stepped out I wrapped one of her towels around me. Too small for my frame, but it
covered what mattered. I rubbed my hair down with a second one.
And then I did the thing I only do when nobody can see me.
I shook. Head, ears, the whole frame of me - one hard sharp shake, the way a dog does coming out
of a lake. Water went everywhere. The mirror got it. The wall got it. A spray hit the ceiling and
came back down in slow fat drops.
I didn't have to do that. That's the part. I could have stood perfectly still; there is not one twitch in
my body I can't override, and I have spent my life proving it in rooms where the proving mattered.
The shake isn't a thing I can't help. It's a thing I pick. Every time. Because it feels good every time,
and in thirteen years of running the numbers on what I let myself have, this is what survived the
cut - eleven seconds of being an animal on a bathroom floor with the door shut.
I shook again. Smaller. Just to feel the ears whip.
The floor was a wet mess. I did not care.
The nightshirt was in the top drawer like she'd said. Soft cotton, faded blue, embroidered along
the collar in a pattern I didn't recognize. On her it had probably come past the knees. On me it
landed a few inches above. The sleeves were short. I pulled it on and worked the fabric over my tail
and let it hang free behind me.
It would do. I wasn't planning on standing up.
* * *
I opened the bathroom door and stepped out into the bedroom.
She was standing in the doorway to the hall with a folded blanket in her hands.
For a long second neither of us moved.
I'd left the bedroom door unlocked. That was mine. I'd assumed she would knock, and I want to be
clear that assuming is not a thing I do - I had gotten comfortable, in a house, in one afternoon, at
the age of twenty-nine, and this is what it cost.
Her eyes went to my ears first. Then the tail. Then back up to my face, to the eyes I hadn't put the
contacts back in for.
The blanket was still folded in her hands.
I stood there in the borrowed nightshirt and tried to make myself smaller. Tail still. Ears down.
Hands at my sides. Tried to take up less of the room than I do.
It didn't work. There's no version of standing in a doorway under a lamp that isn't intimidating
when you're built like this. The ears. The tail. The eyes. The edge of a canine wherever my mouth
isn't all the way shut. Holding still only gave her longer to land on each piece in turn, which I
know, and I did it anyway, because the alternative was moving and moving is worse.
I watched her see it. Her hand tightened on the blanket. Her chin came up an inch. That stillness a
person goes into when their eyes are still catching up to what's in front of them.
Here it was. This part I know.
Not from her - from receptionists, landlords, a doctor or two. The face going polite like a curtain
coming down. The excuse to step into another room. The phone call to my driver ten minutes
later: something's come up, I'm so sorry. The goodbye that means please don't come back,
dressed up nice. I know every beat of it. I've gotten good at making the leaving easy on the person
doing it, which is a skill I acquired on purpose and have not been thanked for.
I just hadn't expected to need the practice on my grandmother.
Then her shoulders came down. The flicker passed. She let out a breath, and when she looked at
me again she wasn't looking at a predator. She wasn't even looking at a stranger.
She was looking at me the way you look at a kid who's come in from the yard with something
scraped up that wants seeing to before bed.
"Oh, sweetheart," she said. Very softly. "Come into the light. Let me look at you properly."
I didn't move.
The catalogue had an answer ready, and the answer was that this was a different kind of goodbye -
the careful one, where she looks me over thoroughly so that later she can tell herself she tried. I've
been told to leave kindly before. The kindness doesn't stop the leaving; it just makes it take longer
and hurt in a different place. So I watched her hands. That's where it shows first. The hands find
something to do that happens to be in another room.
Her hands didn't do that. She was moving toward me.
And the whole frame I had built for what was happening slid sideways and stopped fitting. I'd
been furious - viciously, in the half-second after the door opened, at her for not knocking and at
myself for the lock and at the entire arrangement of my life. Most of that drained out. Not all. I
didn't trust the rest of it yet. I've had this feeling before. It usually breaks.
She didn't come in the way you'd come into a barn with a wary horse in it. She just came in. Set
the blanket on the bed and held a hand out to me, palm up, the way you'd ask a child to come
closer so you could check something.
I went over. My body did it before my head caught up, and I'd like to know what that says, because
I've never done that in my life.
"Before I do this," she said. Quiet. "You should know what you're letting close to you. I'm a doctor,
well, a Pediatrician now. General practice before that. I want to look at the modifications. May I?"
Something in my chest went loose a half-turn.
The look on her face had a name now. That was all it was. I'd read clinical worry as revulsion
because I have never once in thirteen years had cause to build a category for a person who looks at
me that way and wants to help. Not a stranger reaching. Not morbid curiosity. A doctor finding
where it hurts.
I nodded.
She brought her other hand up - slow, asking with her face. I tilted my head down so she could
reach. She touched the outside of one ear. Gentle. The touch you'd use checking the edges of a
scab.
"Does this hurt?"
"No."
I was still half-waiting. The hand on my ear, that close, is where the recoil comes. The little
involuntary pull-back. The face you make when you've touched a thing and only then registered
what it was.
It didn't come.
"Pressure here?" Her thumb pressed lightly at the base of the ear. "Or here?"
"No. The base used to ache after a long day in the hat. Doesn't anymore."
"Mm." She palpated the cartilage along the rim. Slow. Cataloguing. "Has it ever - when it was
happening. Did you get sick? Fevers? Skin trouble where the fur came in?"
"For about a year. Itched mostly. Not after that."
"Mm-hm." Something soft under the clinical. She turned my head a fraction to see the other ear,
thumb tracing just behind the base where the fur meets skin. Worried in the small specific way
you're worried about a thing you might still be able to fix. "Can you move them?"
I flicked one forward. Then the other. Then both.
"Oh, that's clever."
I nearly lost it there.
Clever. That's the wrong word. That's the word you use when a kid shows you a small thing they're
proud of. Nobody has ever applied it to my body. My body is a security problem, an operational
advantage, a thing people have written specimen next to on a chart. It is not clever. There was no
slot for it and I stood there with the word sitting on top of my head like a bird that had landed on
me, waiting for her next sentence to take it back.
"And the tail?" she said.
I moved it once, slow, side to side. Then stopped it dead.
"All right." She let go of my ear and stepped back to take stock of the whole of me, the way you
take stock of a kid's full report once they've finished crying.
And then her face did a thing.
Not the recoil. Something else, and I had no read on it, because I have never gotten this far with
anyone. Her mouth went thin. Her eyes went bright in a way that wasn't softness.
I went very still.
"And you walked into my house," she said, "in a hat. And a dress. And those contacts that hurt
your eyes."
"Yeah."
"All afternoon."
"Yeah."
"Sitting in my chair and drinking my tea and not smiling at me because you didn't want me to see
your teeth."
"Yes."
There it is. Different shape, same animal. Not disgust. Disappointment. I'd taken her hospitality
under false pretenses and let her haul out a photo album for somebody who wasn't in the room.
That's a fair thing to be angry about. I'd have been angry about it. I started getting my body ready
to hand back the half-step she'd let me have, without making it her problem, because that is the
one courtesy I can still offer at the end of these.
Then she spoke again, and I understood that I had the vector wrong.
The bright thing in her eyes wasn't pointed at me. It was pointed past my shoulder. At the world
I'd had to cross to get to her front door. At the version of me she'd had to meet first.
"Sweetheart." The edge was still on it. "You don't ever do that in this house. Do you understand
me? Not in this house. I don't care what you look like under there. I'd have wanted to know you
straight off."
I didn't answer right away. I was checking the sentence for a hook. I went over it twice. That's not
a figure of speech either - I have been handed sentences like that before and they always have
something in them, some small clause that comes due later, and I have learned to find it before I
accept delivery.
There wasn't one.
"All right," I said. Small.
"Sit down, honey. Tell me what happened to you. Slow if you need to."
I sat.
The bed creaked. My tail came around across my lap and I put a hand on it without thinking,
which is a tell, and I know it's a tell, and I did it anyway.
"It was a lab," I said. "When I was sixteen."
She nodded once. Waited.
I waited too. I wanted to give her more. My body wouldn't do it. Every version of this conversation
I've ever rehearsed ends with a door closing politely behind me, and the rehearsals are load-
bearing; I could not get out from under them just because the room was different. I have told the
long version to nobody who didn't already have the file open in front of them.
"That's all I can do tonight," I said. Small.
"Then that's all you need to do tonight."
She meant it. I waited for the look that says otherwise - the kindness with a clock on it. It didn't
come. Her hand stayed loose in her lap. Her eyes stayed where they were.
"I'd have loved you exactly like this," Rosalyn said. "At sixteen. At six. Whatever came off that
table. I want that on the record, since nobody ever gave me the chance to say it out loud."
I had to look at the floor for a minute.

## Chapter 5 - The Letting Go
She didn't leave right away.
She sat down in the little chair by the dresser instead, and it took me a minute to work out that it
was a decision. If she'd stood up and gone, I'd have started crying. She knew that before I did. I
read rooms for a living and I had missed it in my own.
She asked me about the small things.
Did the ears hurt under the hat. Did my tail get cold in winter. Could I eat normal food.
I answered most of them. The contacts were the worst - twelve hours and they felt like sand.
"Then take them out, honey. In this house. Always. And the hat."
I nodded.
Her mouth went a little quirky on one side. "Now. I'll only say this once. The state of that
bathroom floor isn't from the shower, is it."
My face went hot all at once.
"No."
"Mm-hm."
"I was drying off."
"You were drying off?"
"In a particular way."
"Sweetheart, I lived with a man who used to come home from the woods wearing half the mud.
Just maybe put a towel down next time."
I made a noise into the back of my hand - half a laugh, half a wince. The first uncalculated sound
out of me all day.
"There it is," she said, pleased with herself.
The laugh had done something. It took me a while to work out what.
She had made a joke about my body - the water, the mess, the animal fact of me - and the joke
wasn't about whether I should exist. Every one I'd been on the end of since I was sixteen had that
in it somewhere, underneath, holding the thing up. Hers didn't, and the whole structure stayed
standing anyway.
The catalogue went quiet. I hadn't known it could.
I didn't know how long the window would stay open. Windows don't, in my experience. So I was
already deciding to use it - which is not the same as trusting her. I was spending something before
it expired.
"It was a virus," I said. Quiet.
She settled back into the chair instead of standing.
"They had a whole shelf of versions of it. Numbered. The one they gave me was Chimera 9. They
are all different methods of splicing DNA. The other kids got other numbers."
I rubbed at my eye with the back of my wrist.
"Mine took. Most of them didn't. The ones that did sometimes came out worse than I did. Things
that stopped looking like people on the way through. I don't know what was done with those ones
after."
She didn't say anything.
"After the place came down, the agency found me. Put me to work. The dangerous-animals part is
half the truth. The other half is - rich people lose things they'd rather nobody else find. A phone in
a forest. A ring that went over the side of a boat. Strange jobs, weird hours, more money than the
work is worth. The kind of money people pay you to forget about the job once it's done. The rest of
it I can't really talk about."
She was quiet a long moment. She was looking at me the same way she'd looked at me when I
came out of the bathroom - like somebody who'd come in from the yard with something scraped
up. Except this time the scrapes were under the skin. She could see them. She wasn't going to
mention it.
"The biggest hurdle is I can't go outside without a disguise," I said. "People see me and they get
scared. Or they get violent. Or they get scared and call somebody who'll get violent. The hat and
the dress and the contacts - that's how I buy groceries. That's how I get on a bus. That's how I
knock on a front door without somebody phoning it in."
"Mm." She didn't disagree.
She moved closer and tilted my chin up under the lamp. "Let me look at the eyes properly."
I looked. Red into a face that did not flinch.
"Mm-hm. Pupils respond fine. Whole-iris color, not just a ring around the edge - that's a clean
conversion. Any blurriness through the day? Halos around lights?"
"No."
"And the dryness when the contacts come out. Five seconds of saline rinse, next time. Cuts the
recovery."
"Mm."
"Night vision better than mine?"
"Yes." I nodded.
"I'd hope so." A small quick smile. She let go of my chin and stepped back half a step.
"The teeth are more of a challenge. The rest you can hide if you're careful. Can't hide when I bite
food."
"May I see, sweetheart? You don't have to."
I opened my mouth a little. Canines longer than they had any business being, sharp where they
ought to have been rounded.
She looked. She tilted my chin up gently to catch the lamplight. Looked at the gum line. The cusps.
The way the canines sat in the socket. "Do they cut your lip when you talk?"
"No, not really. My mouth seems to have adjusted to the shape of the bite."
"And the rest - open wider for me."
I opened. She looked along the back of the bite.
"All right. The bite's even. That's good news. You'd have known by now if it wasn't - jaw,
headaches, the whole catalogue. The fact that you don't says the rest of your jaw came along with
them."
She didn't look away.
After a long moment she nodded, like she was filing the picture somewhere safe. Then she stood.
Picked the blanket up from the foot of the bed and laid it neater across. Paused at the door.
"Osyra."
"Yeah."
"I'm glad you came. Even as you are."
She closed the door softly behind her.
I lay down. My tail curled around against the small of my back, finding the sleeping curl on its
own. My ears flattened against the pillow.
I had not, in fact, fallen apart.
Nothing in me was bracing. I went looking for it the way your tongue goes to a tooth that's gone,
and it wasn't there, and I was still here.
I'd always assumed the bracing was load-bearing. That it was the thing holding the rest up. It had
come off and nothing had fallen down, and I lay in the dark with that, not liking it as much as I
should have. It's a specific feeling, finding an error in your own work. If I was wrong about that,
I'd been wrong about it every day, in every room, since I was six years old.
The house creaked. Settled. Somewhere down the hall, a kettle clicked off.
I reached over to the dresser, found the comms pin where I'd set it next to the hat, and clipped it
to the base of my ear. Keyed it once.
"Chimera One to Wiki. Checking in."
A pause that wasn't really a pause. "Wiki receiving. You're a day early on sounding human."
"Don't get used to it."
"I won't. Still in one piece?"
"Still in one piece. Staying the night. Maybe longer."
"Noted." A small sound on the line - Vanity, logging it, the way she logged everything. A line in a
record only she kept. "Sleep, Operator. Call me tomorrow."
"Roger."
I unclipped the pin and set it back by the hat.
I closed my eyes.

## Chapter 6 - The Morning
I woke before the sun.
The house had that early quiet - the kind where you can hear the floor settling and the refrigerator
cycling on and off down the hall and nothing else. My ears caught the click of pipes somewhere on
the far side of a wall. I lay there a minute. Tail flat against the small of my back. Ears flat on the
pillow.
I'd slept hard. I can't tell you the last time I slept hard.
I got up the way I move on jobs. Quiet. That one isn't a choice anymore and I've stopped
pretending it is.
* * *
The hall floor was cold. The kitchen was darker than the bedroom - heavy curtains, a north-facing
window. I felt my way to the lamp on the counter and clicked it on low.
The Polaroid was where she'd left it the day before, on the counter next to a ceramic jar of pens.
I looked at it for a while.
She'd offered. I'd put her off. I'd told myself she meant a picture of the version of me she'd been
allowed to meet - the hat, the dress, the careful unsmiling mouth - and I hadn't wanted to hand
her that.
That wasn't the version she meant. I know that now. I knew it the second she said come into the
light, and I'd spent the whole night not looking directly at the fact that I'd been wrong about her
before I'd even met her. I'd had her sorted into a category before the car stopped. Same as
everyone. It's efficient and it's fast and it has never once been wrong in a way that cost me
anything.
It was wrong here. That's the first time. I don't know what to do with a first time.
I picked the camera up. Tested the weight. Checked the cartridge - half a pack of film left. Pulled a
chair out from the table and sat under the lamp.
* * *
The first one was for the record.
I sat straight. Held the camera at arm's length, angled down, framed for head and shoulders. Ears
up. Eyes open. The kind of picture you take when you're documenting something. Shutter. The
camera whirred and spat the film into my hand.
I set it face-down to develop.
The second was a profile. Turned my head left, held the camera further out. The long shape of one
ear from the side, the line of my jaw, the edge of a canine where my mouth wasn't quite closed.
The third - I bit down lightly on the side of one finger and lifted my lip on the other side. Showed
the tooth clean. Like a horse trader. The kind of picture you'd send a doctor who asked.
Three records. Three small pieces of evidence: this is what I am. This is what she accepted.
I set them face-down and let the camera rest in my lap.
Then, without really planning to, I lifted it again.
* * *
The fourth one I almost laughed at while I was taking it.
Two fingers up beside my face. A peace sign. The thing kids do into phones now. Camera up high,
overhead, the way teenagers hold them at bus stops, head tilted a little. Ears asymmetric. One up,
one back.
Shutter. Whir.
The fifth, I blew a kiss at the lens. Pursed mouth. Eyes half-closed. I felt absurd. I kept going.
The sixth was every tooth at once. Full canine display. The smile I have never given anyone in my
life. It came out looking less like a snarl and more like an animal that had just been told a very
good joke.
I looked at that one a long time after it developed.
* * *
I spread them across the table. Six. Three for the record, three for no reason except that I'd wanted
to.
I've been trying since to work out what the last three were, because they weren't documentation
and they weren't for her - she wasn't awake and I hadn't decided to show her. I took them in an
empty kitchen for nobody. There's a word for taking pictures of yourself pulling faces at four in the
morning because it's funny and you're the only one home, and the word is not one that has ever
applied to me.
The peace-sign one was the best. I knew it immediately. Ears uneven. Eyes a little bright. A half-
smile with nothing serious behind it.
It looked like a person. Not an exhibit.
I got up and stuck it to the fridge under a magnet shaped like a pear.
The other five I shuffled into a stack and slid under the body of the camera, face-down. She'd find
them next time she used it. I could see her at this table working through them one at a time, and I
knew which one she'd pick up first and which one she'd put down last, and I was right about both,
which I'll get to.
I left the camera where it had been. Reset. Like nothing happened.
* * *
The coffee maker was the kind with a glass carafe and a metal basket. The bag in the freezer was a
dark roast I didn't recognize. I scooped a generous amount and started a pot.
Eggs in the fridge. A loaf on the counter, half wrapped. Butter left out from the night before. A cast
iron pan on a peg over the stove.
I know how to do this. Hadn't in a long time. Still knew how. Four eggs into a bowl, scrambled
with the back of a fork. Four slices cut and set aside. Burner lit. Pan on. A knob of butter went in
and started to foam.
Six years, maybe seven, since I made breakfast for anyone but myself. Korval doesn't count -
Korval eats when he's told to and not when he isn't. There was a colleague who came by the
safehouse on Saturdays for a while, and once or twice I made him a bacon and egg on toast. Not
the same.
This was the same.
The coffee finished. I poured hers and set it at the far end of the counter, near the doorway from
the hall. The kitchen filled up with butter and eggs and dark roast.
That'd do it, I figured. The coffee, at least.
The chamomile was in the cupboard over the sink, second shelf, in a tin with a hinged lid. I'd
found it while I was looking for the pan and I'd stood there with the lid open longer than a person
needs to.
I made myself a cup. There was a whole pot of the real thing four feet away and a can in my bag
besides, and I made myself a cup of hot water and a flower, because she'd handed me one two
nights ago and I'd been thinking about it since. Nobody has ever once handed it to me. Not in my
own kitchen, not anywhere.
Somewhere down the hall, floorboards.
* * *
I lowered the heat on the pan.
She came in wearing a long flannel robe, slippers, hair tied back loose at the nape of her neck.
Blinked at the lamp. Then at me.
Then at the fridge.
Her hand came up slow and rested on the doorframe. She looked at the photo a long second.
Looked back at me. The corner of her mouth did something.
"Well," she said. "Look at that."
"Coffee's there." I tilted my head at the mug by the door.
"Bless you." She crossed the kitchen and picked it up with both hands, the way old people hold
coffee on a cold morning. Long sip. Eyes closed.
"Strong enough?"
"Right strength." She opened her eyes and went back to the fridge. "When did you take that."
"A few minutes ago."
"Mm." She walked over and stood in front of it. Reached up and touched the corner, gentle, the
way she'd touched my ear the night before.
"You look like somebody who'd taken a picture of herself in a kitchen," she said.
"I was."
"You should do that more often." She turned, mug in hand. "I'll have to find a frame for it."
I didn't know what to say to that.
She went to the counter where the camera was. Set her mug down. Lifted the Polaroid to move it
aside, and the little stack underneath came up against her hand and fanned out across the counter.
She made a small sound. Not surprised. Pleased.
I plated the eggs and buttered the toast and didn't look.
I heard her settle into a chair behind me. The soft sound of photographs being shuffled, examined,
set down one at a time. A small laugh somewhere in there. The kind you make to yourself.
"Sweetheart."
"Yeah."
"This one is a crime."
"Which one."
"The teeth one. The big one. You look like the cover of a heavy metal album."
"That's the kind I figured."
"It's wonderful."
I brought both plates over and set hers down in front of her. She was still holding the bared-teeth
picture between her thumb and forefinger like a tarot card. First one she picked up. Last one she
put down.
"Eat first," I said.
She set it down carefully, face up. "Bossy."
* * *
We ate.
She asked things between bites. Did I cook often. Where had I learned. Did the eggs come out the
way I liked them. Small questions. The kind you ask to keep somebody talking.
I answered. Not many words to most of it. Enough.
Then, not looking up from her plate: "You made a whole pot and you're not drinking any of it."
"It doesn't sit right."
"Mm." She let that be the end of it, which I hadn't expected. There was no follow-up. No is that a
wolf thing. She ate another forkful and I watched her decide not to ask, and I understood that
she'd file it and it would simply be true from now on - that in that house, going forward, there
would be chamomile, and nobody would ever say why.
After a while she set her fork down, picked her mug up, and said, "What happened to you after
that place came down."
I set my fork down too. "Someone from the people I work for now found me. They had paperwork
on me. They offered me a job."
"Just like that."
"Just like that. I'm not really supposed to talk about who or how."
"Mm. I won't ask."
I took a sip. "He brought me a sandwich before he asked me anything else. That part stuck."
She smiled faintly. "Mm. They trained you, after."
"For a while, yeah."
"What was the hard part."
"Learning how to look human again. Don't flick your ears at every loud noise. Smile without
showing your teeth. Don't sniff things." I shook my head a little. "Don't sniff things was the one
that kept catching me out. I'd lean toward something without realizing."
"Did you ever get past it."
"Eventually."
She laughed once. Quietly.

## Chapter 7 - The Letter
A few moments of silence. There was something I wanted to say.
I'd been rehearsing it since the photo went on the fridge. Two hours of it - the wording, the tone,
how to make it land matter-of-fact, which is a thing I was trained to do and am good at. I had a
version that came out clean. Professional. The kind of thank-you you give a colleague who covered
your shift. I'd run it enough times that it was safe.
The silence stretched. I didn't fill it. Neither did she.
I looked at her.
The clean version dissolved.
"Thank you for accepting me for who I am."
It came out small. The last word cracked.
She didn't say anything. She got up. Came around the table. Put a hand on my shoulder.
And that was it. That was all it took, and I'll be honest about how little it was: a hand, on a
shoulder, from a woman who'd known me for nineteen hours.
I have been braced for so long that bracing stopped registering as an activity. It's not something I
do. It's the shape I am. I built the disguise, and the silence, and the whole catalogue of polite
goodbyes for after people see what I am, and every piece of that was engineering - deliberate,
tested, load-rated. It works. It has never failed.
Nothing in it was built for a hand on my shoulder that meant it.
I broke.
It was short and hard and I couldn't stop it, and I did try - I want that much down - that I did the
thing I do, went for the tension, went to take hold of my own breathing the way I've done in worse
rooms than this one, and there was nothing there to take hold of. The controls were not connected
to anything. I have never once in my adult life not been able to do that.
She was worried. She asked if she'd done something wrong. She hadn't. I couldn't get my voice to
work well enough to tell her, which meant for about forty seconds my grandmother sat there
believing she'd hurt me, and I couldn't fix it, and that made it worse, which made it longer.
When it was done I was empty.
That's the only word for it. Something that had been carried was, briefly, not being carried. No
responsibilities. No hiding. Not here, anyway. Not for a minute.
I apologized. I didn't know what else to say. It was the only sentence I had in the drawer that fit.
"What are you apologizing for, sweetheart."
I wiped my face with the back of my wrist. The words came out flat - the debrief voice, because it
was the only one I trusted with this.
"I'm used to being told to leave. Every time. By the third meeting somebody starts looking for a
reason to end it. I came in here expecting that. I didn't have a frame for the other thing."
"Mm."
"I'm not asking you to feel bad. It's just what's true. I came in ready to leave."
She didn't make a thing of it. She kept her hand where it was a long moment, then let it slide off in
a way that didn't feel like leaving.
She sat back down across from me. Picked up her mug. Didn't fill the silence. I've noticed she does
that - she leaves the room for you to walk into, and doesn't stand in the doorway while you decide.
I took a drink of mine. The room had gone brighter, the window letting in enough that the lamp
wasn't pulling its weight anymore. Her eggs had gone cold. She didn't seem to notice.
"Can I ask you something."
"Anything, sweetheart."
I looked at the picture on the fridge a second. Then back at her.
"My mother. My father. The ones in the photo."
She nodded once.
"Are they - " I couldn't find the rest of it.
"Still living?"
"Yeah."
She took her time with the answer.
"As far as I know. Last word I had was a few years back. They settled in the end - bought a small
patch of land up north and put a trailer on it. They grow most of what they eat. There's a kind of
struggling that goes quiet once it stops moving."
I nodded. Didn't trust my voice yet.
"I might have something, though. Let me see."
She got up slow and went down the hall. Past the room I'd slept in. To a door at the end I hadn't
noticed, which I'd like to flag, because I notice doors. That's not a boast, it's a condition. I'd
catalogued that hallway on the way to bed and I had four doors and she opened a fifth, and it
means the catalogue was already coming apart in that house, and had been since the chair.
Hinges complained. A drawer opened. Papers moved. Something heavier - a box - scraped across a
wood floor. The sound a person makes when they're not sure where the thing is, only that it's in
there.
I stayed at the table. Drank what was left of mine. Looked at the photo on the fridge so I wasn't
looking at nothing.
After a while she came back. Slower than she went. An envelope in one hand and a small folded
paper in the other.
"Here." She set them down. "Last letter I got from your mother. Few years old now. It's got a
return address - a P.O. box for a small town up north. They'll be near it. They wouldn't have left
that land." A pause. "She used to write me once a year. Christmas. Then it stopped. I figured she
ran out of things to say. Could mean they're fine."
I picked up the envelope. The handwriting on the front was careful in the way handwriting gets
when paper is a thing you don't waste. I know that hand. Not hers - the type. I've seen it on foster
paperwork, on forms filled out by people who are being careful because being careless costs
money.
"They're proud, mind you," she said. Quiet. "They wouldn't take money. Wouldn't take charity.
They taught themselves how to do everything because they had to - how to grow, how to mend,
how to fix what most people would throw out. If you go to them - and I'm not telling you to or not
to - you bring something they can use, not something that makes them feel small."
"Like what."
She thought about it. "Things you can't pull out of a garden. Salt. Coffee. Spices. Good vinegar.
The little things people stop having when there's nothing left at the end of the month." She looked
at me. "You'd know better than me, once you'd seen them."
I turned the envelope over. The flap was still sealed at one corner where she'd opened it.
"You take it," she said. "It belongs to you more than it belongs to me."
I didn't answer.
I put the letter against my chest. Hadn't planned to. My hand did it, the same way it went across
the kitchen when she held hers out.
She watched me do it and didn't say a word about it.
"Thank you," I said.
"Of course, sweetheart."

## Chapter 8 - The Road North
Korval's car came down the road outside, slow on the gravel.
Rosalyn looked toward the window. Then back at me.
"Oh, sweetheart."
"Yeah."
"I don't have your number."
"I don't have yours."
We both moved at once.
She went for a drawer by the wall phone and pulled out a pad of grocery-list paper. I got my phone
out. Korval honked once outside - polite.
"How fast can you write," I said.
"Faster than you'd think."
She called her number out from memory. Ten digits, slow and clear. I saved her as ROSALYN R,
no embellishment. Then I called mine out, twice, because I didn't trust the first time. She wrote it
down in handwriting that hadn't gotten any less careful since the envelope.
Korval honked again. Less polite.
"He doesn't normally do that twice."
"Then we'd better move."
I slung my bag. She pressed the envelope and the folded letter into my hands.
"Take care of yourself, sweetheart."
"I'll call you."
"You'd better." A small swat at my shoulder.
I want it noted that a woman I met yesterday hit me. Lightly. As a scolding. I have no record of a
relative doing that at any point in my life, and I did not have a reaction ready, and so my face did
nothing at all, which I think she read correctly anyway.
I nodded. Turned toward the door. Got to it. Stopped. Turned back.
"I'll come back when I can."
"I know, honey."
I walked to the car. Korval had the back door open and was looking pointedly somewhere that was
not the porch. I got in. The door closed.
He pulled away.
She was on the porch when I turned to look. She raised a hand. I raised mine. The house got
smaller.
"Good visit?" Korval asked.
"Good visit."
He didn't ask what was in the envelope. He never asks.
* * *
The road toward the highway ran straight a long while, fields on both sides going gold in the late
light. I sat in the back with the envelope in my lap. I'd meant to put it in the bag. My hands hadn't
done it.
I turned it over. The return address in the corner, careful handwriting, a P.O. box for a town I'd
never heard of, somewhere up north. My mother's hand. A few years old, she'd said. They
wouldn't have left that land.
"Korval."
"Mm."
"How far north can this car go before you start complaining."
He met my eyes in the mirror for the first time all day. Considered it. Looked back at the road.
"It is a good car," he said. "It does not complain. The driver, maybe, needs coffee."
"There's coffee on the way."
"Then the car goes as far as you want."
I looked at the address a moment longer.
I could have gone home. That was the whole shape of the thing: I'd come for a grandmother, I'd
got one, the operation was complete and successful and nothing about it required a second act.
Going north was not the assignment. Going north meant knocking on a trailer door to ask two
people why they'd kept me on nothing for six years and then let a nurse's form decide the rest of it,
and I did not have a question prepared, and I have never once walked into a room without
knowing what I was there to get.
I went anyway. I'd like to be able to tell you what I was going for. I couldn't have told Korval if he'd
asked, which is possibly why he doesn't.
Then I clipped the comms pin to the base of my ear and keyed it.
"Chimera One to Wiki."
"Wiki receiving. That's twice in two days you've called when nobody made you."
"I'm extending. Few more days. Maybe a week."
A pause that was Vanity deciding how much to make of it, and deciding on nothing. "Extending
where."
"North. Rosalyn gave me a letter. There's a return address on it." I turned the envelope face-up
against my knee. "My mother. My father. They're up there. Or they were."
"And you're going to knock on that door too."
"I'm going to drive to the town and see if the box is still rented. After that I don't know."
"Then I don't either." Her voice didn't change, but the cadence did - the version of her that writes
things down without being asked. "I'll move your leave to open-ended and keep the desk off you.
Same arrangement. Call me once a day, just so I know you're alive."
"You keep saying that like it's a real risk."
"You keep going to places where it is." A half-beat. "Bring them something useful, Operator. Not
money. Rosalyn's right about that."
I didn't ask how she knew what Rosalyn had said. She always knows more than she's been told; it's
the whole of what she's for, and asking would only make her explain it, and then I'd have to know.
"Roger that."
"Drive safe. Let the man have his coffee."
The pin cooled a degree. Open, idle. I left it.
I slid the envelope into the inside pocket of my jacket, next to the photograph from 1985. The
grandmother, and now the way to the rest of them, riding together over my heart.
"North," I told Korval.
"North," he agreed, and took the on-ramp the other way than home.
The fields gave out to trees. The trees got taller and closer to the road, and the light through them
went the ordinary green of a long evening, and I watched the town where I'd found my
grandmother fall away behind me, and let myself, for once, look forward to the next thing instead
of bracing for it.

## Chapter 9 - The People Up North
The drive took the better part of a day.
The highway thinned as it went, four lanes down to two, two down to a county road with grass
coming up through the seams. The country changed with it. Fields gave out to woods, woods to
low hills, and the towns got further apart and smaller in between, each one a gas station and a
church and a handful of houses that had been good once and were holding on now.
I watched it go by from the back seat. I've seen poor. I've worked in it. But there's a difference
between a city's poor - loud, crowded, fast, a thing happening to people at speed - and this. This is
quiet. This is a place where the struggling stopped moving long enough to put down roots and
grow a garden in it.
A kind of struggling that goes quiet once it stops moving. Rosalyn's words. They fit the window.
Korval didn't talk much on the long stretches. He drank his coffee from the gas station two towns
back and let the road do the talking, and once, when a deer stepped out onto the shoulder and
thought better of it, he slowed without a word and let it change its mind.
* * *
I called Vanity in the early afternoon, somewhere between a town with a name and a town without
one.
"Chimera One to Wiki."
"Wiki receiving. Still got a world."
"Still got a world. Getting close."
"Mm." The sound of her setting it down somewhere only she can read. "The box is still rented, by
the way. I checked. Same name on it as the letter. Renewed eight months ago, in cash, at the
counter."
I closed my eyes a second. Eight months. Recent enough.
"You weren't going to tell me you checked."
"I just did tell you." A half-beat. "I didn't want you driving a day on a dead address. That's all. The
rest is yours to find."
That's the arrangement with her, and it took me years to see the shape of it. She'll pull the thread
that saves you a day. She won't pull the one that saves you the walk. She has never once explained
the difference and she has never once gotten it wrong.
"Thanks, Vanity."
"Call me tonight." And she was gone.
* * *
The town was called Hadley's Fork, and it was a feed store, a diner, a post office the size of a shed,
and a general store that sold a little of everything because there was nowhere else to sell it.
I went in disguised. Hat. Dress. The contacts already grinding by the time I pushed the door open.
The man behind the counter looked up, took me in the way men in small stores take in a stranger,
and went back to his crossword when I didn't turn out to be interesting.
I took my time.
Salt - the good coarse kind, two boxes. A bag of whole coffee beans and a second of ground,
because I didn't know if they had a grinder. Black pepper. A jar of cinnamon and one of
cardamom. A bottle of cider vinegar with some age on the label. A tin of good tea, on a guess about
my mother. Matches. A coil of decent rope, because rope is rope and everyone up here uses it.
Nothing that looked like charity. Everything that looked like a person had thought about it.
I stood in that aisle longer than the shopping needed. It's the only problem I've been handed in
years that I could actually work - a real one, with constraints, with a right answer. Two people I've
never met, too proud for money, and a budget of what fits in a bag. That's a puzzle. I can do
puzzles. I understood while I was doing it that I was hiding in the arithmetic, and I let myself,
because it was the last thing between me and the road out of town.
At the counter I paid cash, like everyone does, and when the man rang it up he said, "Passing
through?"
"Looking for family," I said. True enough to say flat.
"Mm. Lot of family up these roads." He bagged the salt. "Most of it likes to be left alone."
"I'll keep it polite."
He nodded like that was the right answer and didn't ask the name. I'd had a half-dozen ways ready
to not answer. Didn't need any of them.
* * *
The land was four miles out, up a road that stopped being paved and then stopped being graded
and was, by the end, two ruts and a strip of weeds down the middle that hissed along the
underside of the car.
Korval took it slow. The trees pressed close. Then they opened.
A clearing. A trailer set back from the road on cinder blocks, its skirt patched with sheet metal in
three different colors, every patch square and deliberate, done by someone who'd had to learn
how. A garden beside it - bigger than a garden, near an acre, fenced against deer with posts that
didn't match and wire that had been spliced and respliced until it was more splice than wire. Rain
barrels at the corners of the roof. A woodpile, stacked tight and high and covered, that said
somebody here takes winter seriously and meets it head-on every year. A clothesline. A dog run
with no dog in it, the gate standing open.
Everything mended. Nothing new. The whole place a long argument with going without, and
winning it, barely, one season at a time.
I read that clearing the way I read a site, because that's the instrument I have. Entry points.
Sightlines. Where a person would come from. And what it gave me back, item by item, was
competence. Every single thing in that clearing was a problem somebody had solved with their
hands because nobody was coming to solve it for them.
I have been told my whole life that I came from nothing. I was looking at the nothing. It had a
fence around it that had been repaired eleven times.
Korval stopped the car at the mouth of the clearing, well back from the trailer, the way he stops
anywhere he doesn't yet understand. Put it in park. Did not turn the engine off.
"You want me closer."
"No. Here's good. Better they see the car than have it sneak up."
"Mm." He looked at the trailer through the windshield, then at me in the mirror. For once he
didn't look away. "You are not going in as the soldier."
"No."
"As the other one. The salt and the coffee."
"As the granddaughter," I said. "If that's a thing I get to be twice."
He considered that, seemed to find it acceptable, and went back to watching the trees.
* * *
I got out.
I gathered the bags from the seat beside me - salt and coffee and the rest of it, the weight of a
person who'd thought about you before she'd met you - and stood in the ruts a moment and let
myself notice how loud my own heartbeat had gotten.
Smoke off the stovepipe. Thin and pale against the evening. Somebody was home. Somebody was
inside, right now, doing the ordinary thing of keeping warm.
The contacts itched. The hat sat heavy. The dress was wrong for the road and wrong for the place
and wrong, I understood standing there, for the whole of what I'd come to do.
Rosalyn's voice again. You don't ever do that in this house.
But this wasn't Rosalyn's house. This was a door I'd never knocked on, held shut by two people
who gave me away because the world didn't leave them another choice, and I did not know -
standing in the ruts with a bag of salt in each hand - whether they'd open it to the careful stranger,
or to what was under the hat, or at all.
Here is what I had: no plan. Two days ago I'd have called that impossible. I don't move without a
plan; the plan is the thing that keeps the room from happening to me. I had run every version of
this on the drive and every version required knowing what I wanted, and I didn't, and I still don't,
and I got out of the car anyway.
I made myself walk.
Past the garden. Past the woodpile. Up the two cinder-block steps to the door, where a coffee can
of cigarette butts long gone to rust sat on the rail and a windchime made of bent spoons turned
without ringing in the still air.
I set the bags down on the step. Freed my hands.
My fingers went to the brim of the hat. Stopped there. Held it.
I didn't decide. There wasn't time to decide. There was only the door, close enough now to see the
grain in it, and the smoke overhead, and the small ordinary sound of someone moving around
inside - a chair scraping, a cupboard, a voice I couldn't make out saying something to no one in
particular.
I raised my hand, and knocked.

# Part 4 ~ Mireheart
## Mireheart Notes
The Book of Brine - an anomalous artifact, contaminated with a chimera virus.
* * *
The book wants to be read. That is its only need. Its only purpose.
To achieve this, it changes reality. The room grows quiet. The light softens. Time slows just
enough that nothing interrupts you. The world outside the pages stops mattering. The book makes
sure of that.
It wants to be immersive. So it makes itself immersive. By force.
* * *
The Artifact
Waterlogged. Bound in something that used to be hide. The text shifts between readings -
sentences rewriting themselves, margins growing new paragraphs, pages appearing that weren't
there before. Not because the virus does this. The book does this. The book has always done this.
It surfaces every few generations. Pulled from a wreck. Washed up after a storm. Found in an
estate the heirs didn't know belonged to them.
Open it and you don't stop. Not because you can't. Because the book has made the rest of the
world feel like a dream you already left.
* * *
The Virus
Separate. A chimera. Stitched from sources that should not have met.
It contaminated the book at some point - no one knows when. No one knows how. The book
picked it up like a dirty needle. Now they travel together.
The virus does not make you read. The book handles that. The virus waits. It transfers through
skin contact - touching the pages, touching the binding, touching someone else who has touched
it.
The virus changes the body. Scales. Gills. Eyes flattening to the sides of the head. Lungs that start
to crave water. A slow migration from human to something else. Something the bog recognizes.
* * *
Together
The book makes you forget the outside world. The virus rebuilds you for a different one.
The reality warping is the book's doing. The bog creeping into the room - the peat smell, the
standing water, the moss on the walls - that is the virus finding purchase in a world the book has
already softened.
You cannot separate them anymore. The book is the door. The virus is what lives inside.
* * *
Reading aloud
The book wants to be heard. More listeners means more readers. More readers means more
immersion. More immersion means the book gets to be real in more places at once.
The virus spreads through the sound of your voice. Through the moisture in your breath. Through
the scales flaking off your hands as you turn the page.
Whoever hears you wants to come closer. That is the book's work. Whoever touches the book after
you will carry the virus. That is something else.
* * *
The Agency
They have known about the artifact for generations. They have known about the chimera for less
time - it wasn't always there. Someone contaminated the book. Or something. The files are
incomplete.
The protocol contains the book. The book escapes anyway.
The protocol does not account for a virus that lives in the pages and transfers through skin. They
are updating it constantly. They are always behind.
* * *
Inciting incident
A fisherman dredges up the Book of Brine. He doesn't read it - he can't. But he touches it. He
carries it to a museum. He hands it to a curator.
The virus has its first new hosts before anyone opens the cover.
A kid sneaks off during a visit. Finds the Book in a back room. Opens it. The book does its work.
The virus does its work.
The primary gets the case. He is good at it. That is why they sent him.
By the time Osyra is dispatched, the kid is not the only reader anymore, several people who never
read at all are feeling scales under their sleeves, and the primary has stopped filing.
The last report he filed is clean, competent, and correct. She reads it on the way in. It tells her
nothing, which is the first thing that is wrong.
* * *
POV - Osyra
First person, same as the rest. She is the one walking in.
The book opens with her geared up and approaching the zone. Not observing it from outside.
Entering it. The whole story is the walk in and what it costs.
Her instrument is what makes this one work: she reads rooms, she reads sites, she catalogues.
Mireheart is a place where reading is the injury. Every professional habit she has is a way of
getting closer to a thing that only needs you to look at it a little longer.
She knows the protocol: do not read. Do not touch without gloves. Do not get close enough to hear
someone reading aloud.
She has never had trouble not touching things. That is not where she is vulnerable, and she knows
it, and she is right, and it does not help.
* * *
The primary
The Operator already on the case. Male, name TBD. Canon: on the watch list, has read the book
deep, has stopped filing.
He is not the POV. He is what she finds. He is the man in the chair - the one who already made
every decision she is walking toward, in order, and can explain each one, and would make them
again.
His arc happened before she arrived. She reconstructs it from evidence, the way she reconstructs
everything: the gloves on the table. The reports that stop. The peat.
He is not a warning. He is a demonstration. A competent Operator who took each step for a reason
she can follow, and she can follow it, and that is the horror.
* * *
The question
Whether she can pull him out - and whether she can do it without becoming part of the story
herself.
Her defense is control. Mireheart doesn't attack control. It rewards it. The book does not need her
to break; it needs her to keep looking, carefully, professionally, for just a little longer than she
should.
Not a cage that contains her. A cage she inspects her way into.
* * *
Stakes
If no one stops the spread, the world becomes the bog. Peat. Standing water. Dark glassy pools. No
horizon. No dry land.
The book will have what it wants. Total immersion. A reality that is nothing but the story.
The virus will have what it wants. Hosts forever.
* * *
Chapter cadence
Short. Fragmented. The prose drifts as she does.
Her voice is the instrument the story plays. She narrates analytically - that is how she thinks, that
is her whole defense - so the drift shows up as analysis that stops arriving anywhere. The
observations stay sharp. The conclusions stop coming. She keeps cataloguing, correctly, for longer
and longer, about less and less.
She does not notice. The reader does. That is the gap the horror lives in.
Late chapters: she is still describing peat with total precision and has not asked why in six pages.
The last one is written from inside the bog. There is no return.

# Part 5 ~ Redlight
## Red Light Notes
A thought experiment: what a Chimera partner would even look like. Different variant, so the
math doesn't work - that's the point of running it. First person. Unpublished seed for the
romance mini-book.
The bar didn't have a name on the outside, which was the point. Inside, the lights were low and
red, and that was the point too - under red light, red eyes don't show. It's the only room in the city
where I don't wear the contacts. I found it the way I find everything: by following the smell of
people hiding the same thing I am.
He was already at the back when I came in. I knew before I saw him, because the place went quiet
in the particular way a room goes quiet around a predator the other predators respect. A cup of
something untouched in front of him. A stillness I recognized, because it was mine.
When he looked up his eyes were the same impossible red, and slit down the middle like a cat's.
"Chimera-Four," he said. Not a question. "I can smell the wolf on you from here."
"Nine." I sat without being asked.
Neither of us pretended not to look. He didn't flinch at my size. He didn't measure himself against
it either, which is the other thing men do, and the one I've never found a way to answer. He
registered me and went back to being still, and something in my chest I'd kept clamped for a
decade came loose a quarter-turn.
We didn't talk about the labs. That's the etiquette. You don't ask which facility, which guardian
sold you, how many of your cohort came out the other side. You talk around it, in the shorthand of
people who already know the shape of the hole.
"You hide the eyes," he said eventually. "Out there."
"Contacts."
"I wear sunglasses. People think I'm an asshole." A flicker at the corner of his mouth - not quite a
smile, but the place one would go. "Better than the alternative."
I found, to my own irritation, that I'd leaned in.
Up close he smelled of nothing I had to be afraid of. No fear-sweat. No adrenaline. Nobody
bracing to run from me or to win against me. Just a tired person who'd survived the same machine
and come out still able to make a joke about sunglasses.
It was the absence of a thing. That's what got me - not what he was doing, what he wasn't. No
performance. I have spent my whole life in rooms where everybody is performing at me, and I've
gotten so good at reading the performance that I forgot there was a version without one
underneath.
"You're staring," he said.
"You're not running," I said.
"No," he agreed. "Why would I."
That was the whole of it.
Not a confession. Not a promise. I'm not built for those and I don't think he is either, though I
want to be careful there - I don't know that. I know I'm not, and I know he didn't reach for one,
and I have spent an hour deciding those are the same fact and they aren't.
The math I do know. Red eyes met red eyes, and a cat and a wolf make nothing between them but
company. The rewrite made us different species twice over. There's no future here of the kind the
world keeps score by, and I did that arithmetic before I sat down, the way I do all of it, and I sat
down anyway.
Just this. A red room. Two survivors. The rare and ordinary relief of being a person somebody
looked at and didn't look away from.
I slid the contacts case across the table and let him see me do it. Let him watch me decide, in front
of him, not to hide.
That was the only thing I did all night that I'd call a choice. Everything else I could explain - the
bar, the sitting, the leaning in. Not that. That one I made on purpose and I still couldn't tell you
what I was buying.
He picked up his cup.
Outside, the city kept insisting on its rules, on its breeding and its belonging and its lists of what
counts. In here, under the red light, none of it could reach us, and for one held breath that was
enough.
I'd leave first, later. I always do.
But not yet.
