### Chapter 6 - I Know Your Names

Nine days in Saltmarrow, which is long for me. You don't interrogate a frightened town. You ask them about themselves and they hand you everything. I asked the harbormaster, Toll, about his rope-burned hands and let him talk for an hour. I learned the widow Serafine's drowned boy was named Eddan. I learned the innkeeper's wife was pregnant and hiding it from a husband who drank. I keep things - the lab tested my memory the way you test a rope, with load, until they knew what it held, and now it holds whether I want it to or not.

It worked. It always works. They gave me the docks and the tide tables and the honest count of the missing, and I took the eel-thing they called the Drownfather in the shallows and held its jaw open until the charge in its throat went.

They didn't turn on me. Saltmarrow was grateful, they waved me off the dock, and this is not that kind of story. It's smaller and it's worse.

You cannot learn a person for the length of a mission and then set them down. I have tried. The interest is a tool when you pick it up and it is not a tool you get to put back, because to learn someone is to decide they matter, and that decision doesn't have an off. I meant to learn Toll's hands the way you learn a lock - functionally, to get the door open. But you can't spend an hour inside a man's forty years and come out with only the parts you needed. I came out with Toll. I came out with Eddan, a boy I never met, drowned before I got there, whose name I will have until I die because I learned it to comfort his mother for one afternoon and there is no procedure for giving a name back. I have a dead child in my head that I picked up as a lever and cannot put down.

Every town, nine days, a lock's worth of people learned and not one of them returnable. I am carrying hundreds. The baker's girl afraid of the church. The innkeeper's wife's secret, which was never mine and is mine forever now. A man in a valley three years ago who fed stray cats and is certainly dead and lives in me because I asked him one kind question to get a map. I did not keep these people because I loved them. I kept them because the job made me learn them and learning is a door that only opens.

Saltmarrow was grateful. I want that on the record, because this isn't a chapter about people turning on me. They waved me off the dock. Toll shook my hand with those ruined fingers and meant it.

And I went back four years later on another contract, and I asked him how the hands were in the cold, and I watched him not remember telling me. Worse than not remember - I watched him work out that I must have read it somewhere. A file. Something the Agency keeps. He got polite very fast. Because a man tells a stranger about his hands and it's an hour of his life, and finding out the stranger *kept* it, exactly, four years, is not warm. It's surveillance wearing a friendly face, and he wasn't wrong, that's the thing, that is precisely what it was when I picked it up. I learned his hands the way you learn a lock.

Nobody wants to be in a wolf's head. Serafine would be sick to know I still have Eddan. She gave him to me on a bench for one afternoon because she needed to say his name to something that would sit still for it, and she went home and got on with a life that has room in it for other things, and I drove out with her son and he is still in here and he was six.

There's nobody to be angry at for it. It works perfectly. It saves the town every time. And every time it works it puts another handful of people into a house that only I live in, room after room of the saved and the incidental and the already-dead, all of them learned on purpose, none of them coming to visit.

Eddan. Six years old, drowned in Saltmarrow before I ever got there. I never saw his face. I'll have him until I die.

Nobody did that to me. I reached for a lever and the lever was a person.
