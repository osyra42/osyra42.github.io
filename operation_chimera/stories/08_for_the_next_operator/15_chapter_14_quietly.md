### Chapter 14 - Quietly

His name was Petrun. Nineteen, maybe. Local, one of six volunteers the town of Threshfield threw at me when I came for the thing living under their grain silos - a burrower, Vanity's file called it a marrow-hound, though the word does no work explaining it. Long as a truck. No eyes worth the name. It hunted by the drum of feet on soil and it had already taken four field hands, pulled straight down through the dirt so fast the men beside them said the ground just swallowed a person mid-sentence.

Fast, brave, useless. The kind who thinks courage is a substitute for doing what he's told. You get one in every town that sends volunteers. On the first sweep he broke my line. I had them spaced, silent, moving heel-to-toe the way you move when the thing under you listens for weight. Petrun ran ahead. Wanted the first sight of it. Wanted to be the one. He stamped forty meters of open field like he was putting out a grass fire, and the marrow-hound came up under a man named Coll who had done everything right.

It came up as a wet unfolding. The soil heaved and split and the hound's mouth was already open when it cleared, a ring of hooked plates rotating the wrong way, and it took Coll from the hips down in the first bite. He stayed talking. That is the part they never believe when I write it plain, so I will write it plain: the lower half of him was gone into that grinding ring and he was still upright from the waist, hands out, asking us what happened, asking it in a normal voice, until the blood pressure dropped out of him and he folded sideways into the hole his own legs had gone down. The hound pulled the rest after. The field closed over it like water.

Petrun stood there with Coll's death on his boots and I watched the recruit's instinct arrive too late in his eyes.

When a man has done something reckless that got somebody killed, you don't correct him in front of the others. You don't make the group watch you break him. You take him aside, and before you cut, you find the true thing you can praise, because there is always one, and you lead with it. That's the method and it is a good method.

I walked Petrun behind the silo where no one could hear. I told him his speed was real and rare, that I had seen trained men slower, that on the right day that speed saves a line instead of ending one. All true. Then I told him, low, that the ground listens, that his feet had spoken for all of us, that Coll died of Petrun's courage and not his cowardice - and that this was fixable. That I needed him behind me on the next sweep, heel-to-toe, and that if he gave me that I would not say a word to the others about what happened in the field.

He took it. That is the thing. He did not get loud, did not get proud, did not have to save face because I had left him his face. He nodded and his throat worked and he said yes, Operator. The next sweep he moved like a man walking on his own held breath. We put the hound down that night - I opened the ground with charges and took its head with the line spacing Petrun kept perfectly, no one in front of me, no one behind me leaving. Six volunteers went home. Threshfield kept its silos.

I did it right. I did it so right that not one person besides Petrun and me knew there had ever been a correction at all.

That's worth holding next to the rest of it.

Two weeks later Vanity pulled the local accounts for the debrief, the way she does, and read one back to me flat because she knew I would want it flat. Petrun had talked. Not about his running. Not about Coll. In his telling the monster had cornered him alone behind the silo, in the dark, out of everyone's sight, and put its voice down low against his ear and told him what it would do to him if he stepped wrong again. He showed people how close I had stood. He was believed instantly, because of course he was. A six-foot-four thing with a wolf's jaw takes a boy behind a building and speaks low - there is exactly one story the human mind wants to make out of that, and it is never the true one.

The privacy was the mercy. It's also the only thing his version needed, and those are not two facts. They're one fact.

There's no third door. I've looked. Correct him in front of the others and you humiliate him and he hates you and the hate has forty witnesses. Correct him alone and you spare him and you have just built an unwitnessed room, and an unwitnessed room will hold any story a frightened nineteen-year-old needs to put in it. What he needed was for Coll's death to have come from somewhere other than his own feet, and there I was, the correct shape, standing close enough to have whispered.

I don't think he set out to lie. I think he walked out from behind that silo with something he could not carry and found, over about a week, that the story got easier every time he told it, and the easier version had the monster in it. He probably believes it now. It would be strange if he didn't.

Six volunteers went home. Threshfield kept its silos. Coll's widow got a pension off the report I filed, which named the marrow-hound and the ground conditions and did not name Petrun, because I'd given him my word behind that silo and I keep those.

So there's a boy in that town who tells a story about the night a wolf got him alone in the dark, and the only part of it that's true is that I told him his speed was real and that I meant it.
