### Chapter 17 - The People You Could Be

*Hand a person the better version of themselves and some will reach for it. Most will look at it, and set it down, and pick up the knife instead.*

The town was called Tidegate, a river crossing gone to rust, and the thing killing its people came up out of the water at night. A gillheart - long, pale, hooked jaw, eyes gone milk-white from living under the ice. It took four before we got there. It dragged them under and it fed slow. We found the fourth still tangled in the reeds with most of her ribs opened out like a book. That is the plain fact of it. Her name was Ada Voss and she had been a schoolteacher and she was thirty-one and there was not enough of her chest left to close her coat over.

The town had a foreman by then. Man named Coll. He had organized them, and I want you to understand I was glad of it when I walked in, because organization is rare and it is the thing that keeps people alive. He had them rationed and watched and he had a plan for the crossing. He was frightened and mean with it, and he had started deciding which of them were worth the food.

When you want a person to be better than they are, you tell them they already are. Not flattery. You name the finer self out loud, hand it over like it was always theirs, and make them ashamed to be less than the thing you just called them. It works on the worst nights on the worst people. I told Coll he was the reason this town still had a count to take. I told him a lesser man would have already run the ferry and left the slow ones on the bank, and that everyone here could see he was not that man. I said it flat. I said it like it was already true.

And he reached for it. I watched him do it. He straightened. He put the two he had written off back on the ration list. He waded out with me to the reeds and helped me carry Ada Voss up the bank in a tarp when his own people would not touch her, and his hands shook the whole way and he did not stop. That is what the principle buys you. For one night I gave a mean, scared man a better man to be, and he stood in it.

That's real. I want to be careful here, because everything after this is going to sound like I'm saying it wasn't.

Because the gillheart came back the second night, and it came up under the ferry where the children slept warm, and there was a moment - one - where the plan needed a body in the water to hold the winch line while the rest hauled. Cold black water, the milk-eyed thing somewhere in it. I told Coll to hold the line. I told him the man I had met would hold it.

He looked at me. He looked at the water. And he took a girl - a foster girl, no people to raise a noise, twelve maybe, the kind nobody counts - and he put the line in her hands and he pushed her off the boards himself.

I got her out. I got the gillheart too, both barrels through that pale hooked skull, and it thrashed and voided black in the shallows and went still and the town was saved. The girl lived. She has a scar around both wrists now like she wore the rope forever.

And Coll - I did not touch him. I want you to know that. I stood over him dripping and I gave him the last clean chance. I said, *that is not who you are.* One more time. I held out the better man.

He said, *she was nobody. You'd have done the same.*

*Hand a person the better version of themselves and some will reach for it. Most set it down and pick up the knife, because the knife asks nothing of them but somebody else's blood.*

Two sentences and both of them are load-bearing. The first one is the arithmetic - he had run the count on every person on that ferry long before I got there, and a foster girl with nobody to raise a noise came out lowest, and he did not have to think about it when the moment came because the thinking was already done. That's what a ration list is. He'd been doing it in his head for a week and calling it management.

The second sentence is the one I keep. *You'd have done the same.* He needed that. Not as an excuse - he wasn't asking me to forgive him, he was informing me. Because if I'd have done the same, then there's no better man, there never was one, the thing I held out to him on the bank was a thing nobody actually is, and he hasn't fallen short of anything. He'd rather live in a world with no finer self in it at all than live in one where there is and he didn't reach it.

That's it. That's the whole of what I've learned about people and it took me nine years. It isn't that they can't be better. Better costs, and it costs most exactly when it counts, and cruelty is free and fast and it's *warm* - and the worst part, the part I did not expect at sixteen or at twenty-five, is that afterward they will rebuild the world in their heads into one where nobody could have done otherwise. They don't carry it. They edit it. Coll went to bed that night in a town he'd saved.

I was sold to a lab at sixteen by a guardian with every chance to be better who found it too expensive. So I know the arithmetic from the inside. I was the lowest count in that house too, and I'd bet the money that she has a version now where there wasn't any other choice, and that she sleeps.

So. Nine years, and here is everything I've got.

I've read back through these and they're a catalogue of people at their worst, and I know what that reads like. It reads like somebody building a case. So let me put the other column down, because I've been keeping both and it isn't as one-sided as the pages make it look.

The woman with the notebook stayed up nights for a town that never listened to her once. Nobody made her. Coll waded into the reeds and carried Ada Voss up the bank when his own people wouldn't touch her, and his hands shook the whole way, and he didn't stop. Ezren drew that mine in the dirt from memory and got every branch right. Petrun walked the next sweep heel-to-toe on his own held breath. Serafine sat on a bench and gave me her drowned son's name because she thought I might need it. That is also nine years. Those are also the same people.

Here's the closest I've come to squaring it.

I keep expecting them to be one thing. That's my error, not theirs. I show up after the worst night of their lives and I take a reading and I write down what I find, and I have been treating that reading as the answer to *what are people*, when what it actually measures is what a person does at hour fifty of being terrified with no one coming. Nobody is at their weight class then. I've watched what fear does to a body and I have never once seen it improve one.

They aren't cruel. Cruel is a decision and most of them never got as far as deciding. They're frightened, and frightened wants two things - it wants the fear to have a shape it can hold, and it wants that shape to be somewhere other than inside itself. So it goes into a list, or a notebook, or a neighbor with odd hours, or a mother who let her boy fish past the buoys. And when I take the monster away, the fear doesn't leave with it. It has nowhere to go and it finds the nearest object, and about half the time the nearest object is me, and I would like to record that this is not personal even though it has never once felt that way.

Then the sun comes up and they have to be people again, with the things they did still in the room. Nobody can carry that. So they put it down. They edit. By the second day there's a version where the list was fair, or the Deacon held the high ground, or a wolf got a boy alone behind a silo - and they're not lying to me, they've genuinely got the new one now, and the old one is gone in a way I don't think can be undone from outside.

That's the whole of it. Not evil. Frightened animals, briefly, and then embarrassed animals forever, and the embarrassment is the part that turns and bites, because I'm the only one still standing there who remembers.

Knowing all that changes nothing about what it's like. I want to be honest with you about that too. I understand exactly why Pruitt did it and I still had to drive four hours with it.

There's one thing that would work, and I've thought about it on every job I've ever run. If I stood aside. If I let the gillheart come, let the milk-eyed things rise in every town, one shared horror with teeth for all of them - they'd hold each other's lines. They'd be decent. Nothing on this earth unites the small and the frightened like something worse at the door. I could hand them each other, whole, and all it would cost is me stepping back and some number of them I'd never have to count.

I've had the arithmetic ready for years. It's good arithmetic.

I won't.

Not because it wouldn't work. Because it's the same sentence Coll used on the boards - *she was nobody, you'd have done the same* - and I would be saying it about a whole town instead of one girl, and the only difference between us would be the size of the number.

So I go in front. It doesn't mean anything; there's no meaning out there waiting to be found, only what a person picks up and declines to set down. Everyone else at that table put theirs down because it was heavy. This is mine: nobody goes past me into the dark. Not the town that calls me a monster, not the girl with rope scars around both wrists, not the foreman who'd drown her twice.

They will not be better. I go anyway. That's the only thing in nine years that was ever mine to assign.

I don't know why I wrote this down. I've said that already. But I notice I finished it, and I notice I put a name on the front, and you're the one standing there.

Hold the line. It's yours now.

- Osyra
