# Chapter 7 - Talk Yourself Calm

Cinderslope was a mining strip on a dead mountain, and the thing coming down the shafts folded its jaw outward like a hand opening and took nine men off the lower galleries before we got the call. I went down to seal the last one and found a survivor at the bottom, a shift foreman named Harl, wedged in a supply nook behind the blasting mats, and my lamp found the rest of the gallery in the same sweep that found him.

It hadn't finished. It had been interrupted mine after mine, so the gallery was a half-eaten meal held in the cold - a man still in his harness with everything below the ribs drawn out down the incline in a long wet rope, another folded backward over an ore cart the wrong way, a third who'd crawled a distance on what still worked and wrote the distance on the floor behind him. Harl had been down there with that for eleven hours. Lamp for the first three, then the dark, then just the sounds, because a mountain won't let a body cool quietly.

He was past screaming, into the flat low shaking. You can't pull a man out of that - pull and they lock. So I sat down in the muck at his level and put the rifle on the ground where he could watch me do it, and shut up, and let the silence get heavy enough that he filled it.

He filled it. A person doesn't talk himself calm by reciting facts; he calms by confessing, by finding the one thing he can't hold alone and handing it to another mind so he isn't the only one carrying it. The steadying and the unburdening are the same motion. There's no version of the first without the second.

So what came out of Harl was the heaviest thing, because the heaviest thing is what has to come out for a heart rate to drop. When the jaw opened the first time he had put his hand flat on the back of a young miner named Ost and shoved, and bought the nook with him. He watched Ost go into the opening hand of that mouth. He told me because he had to tell someone or stay a held breath forever, and I was the someone, and it worked. Hundred-ten, ninety, seventy. He walked out of that mountain on his own feet.

I did that. The listening did that. It was kind and it was competent and it saved him.

And it damned me, and it was never going to go any other way.

Harl walked out of that mountain with the hand-on-Ost's-back outside of himself now. That's what the calming *is*. But a man can't go home and build a life on top of a murder that's sitting fully known in the head of something the county already calls a monster.

He didn't calm down and then decide to betray me. Follow the order of it, because the order is the whole thing. The confession came out of him and went into me and his pulse dropped, and that same night he had a new unbearable thing, which was that his guilt was now in a witness. One way to survive that. Make the witness unbelievable.

At the inquest he described the thing in the dark. He built me out of the same pictures he'd handed me in the nook - the wet rope down the incline, the man folded over the cart - arranged so the jury couldn't tell what ate the gallery from what sat in it with him. Not malice. The identical instinct that brought his heart rate down: take the unbearable thing out of yourself and put it into a shape the world throws away. He'd already done it once that week and it had worked.

I was the second place he put it.

The kindness built the knife and then required its use. Every good thing I did in that nook was load-bearing for what he did on that stand.

There's no clean version of that night. If I'd stayed quiet and let him lock up he'd have died down there and taken Ost with him, and eleven hours of a man screaming in the dark would have been all the account Ost ever got. I chose to save Harl. Saving him meant receiving it. Receiving it meant I had to be made unbelievable, because a confessor with my face cannot be left standing anywhere near a man's new life.

He was not ungrateful. I want to be exact about this, because ungrateful is the easy word and it's wrong. He was grateful the whole time he was doing it. Both of those were running in him at once on that stand and I don't think he could have told you which was which.

Ost is in me now too. Harl handed him over and went home.
