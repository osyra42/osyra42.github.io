# Chapter 15 - He Held the Line

The town was called Deepgallery, a mining settlement at the bottom of a valley that funneled cold air down like water. Something had come up out of the old shafts. We called it a delver in the debrief because it had no proper name yet - a blind, boneless thing the length of a train car, skin like wet paper stretched over cartilage, and a mouth that ran a third of its body. It fed on heat. It found you by the warmth coming off your skin and it opened you up to get to the warmer parts inside.

I had a support element assigned that night. Local militia, twelve men, folded under my command for the duration. Their sergeant was named Halloran. Broad, loud, the kind of man who fills a room with his voice so no one notices he is standing at the back of it.

When the delver came through the eastern barricade, Halloran ran.

I saw it. Clearly, from thirty feet, with the lamps still up - I say that because a lot of this job is deciding later what you actually saw, and this is one of the few I've never had to argue with myself about. He dropped his weapon and he ran, and two of his men who were watching him for their cue almost ran with him. I put myself between them and the dark. The delver took a miner named Oster who had not run - lifted him, folded him, and I heard the sound a rib cage makes when it is turned inside out, a wet unbuttoning, and then Oster stopped making any sound at all while the thing was still eating the warm parts of him. I put four incendiary rounds into the soft length of it and it came apart in the cold and burned.

We held. Barely. We held because the two men who almost ran did not run, and they did not run because I was in front of them.

Afterward Halloran came back. He had a story ready by then. He said he had circled to flank it, that he had been the one to draw it off the western wall so I could get my shot. His men looked at me. They knew. He knew I knew.

I let him have it.

I let a frightened man keep the version where he held the line, and I'd defend the reasoning to anyone. A unit that watches its sergeant get shamed in the cold stops being a unit. Halloran's men needed him to be the man he was pretending to be, and I could give them that for free. I said, in the debrief, in front of them, that Sergeant Halloran's flank maneuver had bought me the shot. Vanity recorded it exactly as I said it. His shoulders came down from around his ears. His men stood a little straighter. Morale is not a soft thing. Morale is the difference between the two men who stayed and the corpse of the one who was too slow.

It worked. It always works. What it turns into afterward is the part nobody warns you about.

The report I signed went up the chain. It named Halloran. It used my callsign as the corroborating witness, which meant it carried weight, because whatever else they think of us, they do not think we lie about the field. My mercy had a signature on it and the signature was mine.

They promoted him.

Not once. The story I gave him was good, and a good story climbs. Two years later Sergeant Halloran was Director Halloran, running Containment for the whole northern district, and Containment is the office that decides which Chimerans get a leash and which get a bullet. The office that decides whether a thing like me is an asset or a specimen. I read his name at the top of a directive that reclassified three of my own kind - operators, people who had bled for the same chain of command - as unacceptable retention risks. Two of them I had trained. He signed it with the same hand he used to drop his rifle in the dark at Deepgallery.

I built that man. I took a coward and gave him a spine made out of my own credibility, and he used the reach to sign away three of my own kind.

The face you let a man save doesn't stay a face. It becomes standing, and standing becomes a room and a title, and there is no mechanism anywhere in the chain for the truth to catch up with it, because the only witness who could correct the record is the one who put the lie in it. I can't unsay it. The moment I try, every word I've ever filed goes soft, including the ones that got people tiered and contained correctly, and he knows that, and he has known it since the night I said it.

Here's the part that keeps me up. I know precisely why he did it. A man who ran in the dark and got given a medal for it spends the rest of his life around people who might remember the running. There were twelve of them at Deepgallery, and eleven went home to a mining town, and one of them was a Chimeran with a perfect memory and a callsign on the report. He didn't reclassify my kind because he hates us. He did it because we're the last people alive who know what he is, and he finally got the office where you can do something about that.

I could have let him drown in the cold. One true sentence in the debrief and Halloran ends as a discharged militiaman, harmless, small. I was decent instead. I remembered being sixteen and sold in front of people who could have said something and stayed quiet, and I would not do that to a man, not even that man, not even once.

Mercy doesn't know who deserves it. It goes where you point it and you point it at whoever is bleeding in front of you, and Halloran was bleeding in front of me in a cold valley at four in the morning, and that was all the qualification the thing required.

Two of the three he reclassified I trained myself. One of them is alive. I'm not going to write down where.

I let him keep the line he never held.

He held it against me.
